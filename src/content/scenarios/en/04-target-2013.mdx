## Executive summary

Between November and December 2013, attackers entered Target's network using the
credentials of a Pennsylvania refrigeration company, reached the checkout
registers, and pulled 11 GB of card data out to a server in Eastern Europe. Up to
110 million customers affected.

What makes this case required reading is not the technique — a McAfee executive
described the malware as "absolutely unsophisticated and uninteresting". It is that
**the detection systems worked.** FireEye alerted. Symantec alerted. Nobody acted.

**Key metrics:**

- Time from first access to public confirmation: 37 days
- Automated alerts raised and not acted on: at least two rounds from FireEye, plus one from Symantec
- Data compromised: 40 million cards and 70 million personal records
- Volume exfiltrated: 11 GB
- How Target found out: a phone call from the Department of Justice

<Warn />**Real case, public sources.** Everything that follows comes from the March
2014 majority staff report of the U.S. Senate Commerce Committee and from the
reporting that report cites. Where the source marks something as unconfirmed, it
appears as unconfirmed here too — telling the proven from the probable is part of
the craft.

---

## Why this case still matters

Target was certified PCI-DSS compliant in September 2013, two months before the
intrusion. Its chief financial officer told Congress the company had "multiple
layers of protection, including firewalls, malware detection software, intrusion
detection and prevention capabilities and data loss prevention tools".

All of that was true. And it was not enough.

The Senate report analysed the incident using Lockheed Martin's *intrusion kill
chain* model, and its conclusion is what gives this case its title: there were
seven links where the chain could have been broken, and it was broken at none of
them.

### A note on the dates

The Senate report puts the first network access on **12 November**, citing The New
York Times. Krebs, with sources close to the investigation, says **15 November**.

Nobody is wrong here: these are two sources with access to different investigators,
publishing about a forensic investigation that was still open. I keep the 12th in
the chronology because it is the date in the official report, but the discrepancy
is recorded here deliberately.

It is a habit worth having: **when two good sources disagree, you note the
disagreement instead of choosing in silence.** A report that promises precision it
does not have is worse than one that marks its grey areas.

---

## 1. The vendor

Fazio Mechanical Services is a refrigeration company from Sharpsburg,
Pennsylvania, specialising in supermarkets. It had remote access to Target's
network for electronic billing, contract submission and project management.

Two details from the report explain why it became the way in.

**Fazio used the free version of Malwarebytes Anti-Malware.** That version has no
real-time protection and its licence covers personal use only, not commercial. An
email with a malicious attachment met nothing that would stop it.

**Target rarely required two-factor authentication from its low-level
contractors.** According to a former vendor manager at the company. The stolen
credential was enough to get in.

There is a third detail, less comfortable: per Krebs's analysis, the attackers may
have found Target's vendor list through ordinary internet searches. The supplier
portal and the facilities management pages were public, and the metadata in the
documents published there made it possible to map the internal network before
touching it.

<Warn />The MITRE technique that describes this, T1199 (Trusted Relationship),
explicitly lists HVAC contractors among its examples. This case is the reason.

---

## 2. From billing to the registers

Here is the jump the Senate report cannot fully explain, and it is worth saying so
with the same honesty: **the public reporting does not make clear how the attackers
moved from the external billing system to the point-of-sale terminals.**

What is known:

- Fazio very likely had access to Target's Ariba billing system.
- That external portal was probably not fully isolated from the rest of the network.
- Once inside, the attackers could have taken advantage of the name of a default
  account from a BMC Software management product to move around.

All three statements are conditional in the source, and they stay conditional here.
What is not conditional is the outcome: an air conditioning vendor ended up being
the road to the card data of 40 million people.

**That is the architectural lesson.** A vendor billing portal has no reason
whatsoever to have a path toward the register network. Segmentation is not an
optional good practice: it is what turns an incident at a vendor into an incident
confined to that vendor.

---

## 3. The malware

What went onto the registers was an adapted version of **BlackPOS**, available on
underground forums for somewhere between 1,800 and 2,300 dollars.

How it works is simple. When a card is swiped, the magnetic stripe data travels
unencrypted through the terminal's memory before being sent to the payment
processor. The malware reads that memory at exactly that moment. This is called
*RAM scraping* and it exploits no vulnerability: it takes advantage of a window in
which the data has to be in the clear in order to be processed at all.

A second piece of malware, installed on an internal server, moved the stolen data
across the network and through the company firewall. That component **was disguised
as a legitimate BMC Software product**.

On the question of sophistication the sources disagree, and that is worth
recording: McAfee's director of threat intelligence described it as "absolutely
unsophisticated and uninteresting", while the director of the Department of
Homeland Security's cybersecurity centre called it "incredibly sophisticated". When
two qualified sources say opposite things about the same artefact, it is usually
because they are measuring different things: the code itself, and the operation as
a whole.

FireEye's system did not just alert: it also **decoded the destination servers**
where the stolen data was piling up for days. The information needed to cut off the
exfiltration was sitting in the console.

On top of that, the software had an option to automatically delete detected
malware, and that option **was turned off**.

### Why this happens

It is easy to read this as negligence and stop there. It is more useful to
understand the mechanism, because it is the same one in any SOC.

The Senate report points at a telling detail: the FireEye alert arrived under the
generic name `malware.binary`. And it adds a careful hypothesis — it is possible
the team treated it as a false positive if the system alarmed frequently.

That is the real problem. **An alert without context, in a queue full of alerts
without context, is indistinguishable from noise.** Detection did not fail: what
failed was the ability to separate signal from noise, which is a problem of
process, of tuning and of workload — not of tooling.

It is the exact same mechanism as case 02, where an *impossible travel* alert with
a high false positive rate was nearly closed wrongly. The difference is the scale
of the consequence.

---

## 5. The exfiltration

The data left over **plaintext FTP** to external servers, at least one of them in
Russia. Eleven gigabytes across two weeks.

Two details from the Dell SecureWorks analysis show the attackers were thinking
about detection:

**They exfiltrated between 10:00 and 18:00 central time.** In the middle of
business hours, so their traffic would get lost in the normal volume of a retail
chain in peak season.

**They used compromised staging servers inside Target's own network** to pile up
the data before shipping it out.

On the defensive side, the report names one concrete measure that would have cut
this off: an allowlist of authorised FTP destinations. A connection from Target's
network to a server in Russia had no legitimate reason to exist.

---

## 6. How Target found out

On 12 December, the Department of Justice called Target.

Executives told Congress they had no knowledge of the breach until that moment. The
company confirmed the problem on 15 December, and on the 18th the story was
published by Brian Krebs, an independent journalist, before Target said anything.
The official announcement came on the 19th.

The order is worth pausing on: **the internal alerts had been there since 28
November, and the notification that actually landed came from outside fourteen days
later.** This was not a visibility problem. It was a problem of what gets done with
what you can already see.

## Lessons learned

**Third-party access is access.** The question is not whether the vendor is
trustworthy, but what they can reach from wherever you plug them in. Fazio had no
business being able to get anywhere near the registers.

**Certification is not security.** Target was certified two months earlier. An
audit verifies controls at a point in time, against a list; it does not verify that
somebody looks at the alerts on a Thursday night.

**A detection nobody acts on does not exist.** That is the central lesson. Buying
the tool is the easy part and the part that fits on a slide. Sustaining the process
that turns an alert into an action is the expensive, boring part that is hard to
justify until something happens.

**Turning off automatic response is a decision, and it has to be documented.**
Somebody switched off FireEye's automatic deletion. They may have had good reasons —
fear of it deleting something legitimate in production is reasonable. What cannot
happen is that decision arriving without a matching commitment to manually review
whatever the system flags.

**The name of the alert matters.** `malware.binary` tells nobody anything at three
in the morning. An alert that communicates neither its severity nor what to do
about it competes at a disadvantage against the rest of the queue.

---

## Recommendations

- Segment the payment network from everything else, and in particular from
  anything reachable by third parties
- Require two-factor authentication for all vendor access, regardless of the
  contractor's tier
- Apply an allowlist of outbound destinations: no connection to a country
  with no legitimate operation
- Review what vendor and infrastructure information is public, and strip the
  metadata from published documents
- Periodically audit which automatic responses are switched off, and why
- Track the rate of unattended alerts as a metric of its own: if nobody
  measures it, nobody knows it is growing
