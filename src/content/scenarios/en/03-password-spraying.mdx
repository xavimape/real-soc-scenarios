## Executive summary

Almost every organisation runs two controls against password guessing: a
correlation rule that alerts when one account piles up failures, and an automatic
lockout that freezes the account once a threshold is crossed.

This case is about an attack that walks past both without triggering either. It
does not evade them with anything clever: it walks past because **both of them
count failures per account, and the attacker never accumulates failures on any
single account.** One common password is tried against two hundred and eighty-seven
different accounts, once each.

What did produce an alert was the MFA prompt on one account whose password the
attacker guessed correctly. What produced no alert at all was the account that
actually got compromised, because it was excluded from that policy.

**Key metrics:**

- Attack duration before the first alert: 6 hours 12 minutes
- Target accounts: 287 out of 1,412 in the directory
- Maximum failed attempts against any single account: 2
- Brute force rule threshold: 10 failures in 15 minutes for one account
- Correct passwords found: 2
- Attacker session on the compromised account: 5 hours 39 minutes
- Primary technique: T1110.003 (Brute Force: Password Spraying)

<Warn />**Synthetic scenario.** The organisation, the accounts and the logs were
built for the exercise. IP addresses come from the ranges RFC 5737 reserves for
documentation, the domains use the `.example` TLD from RFC 2606, and the autonomous
system numbers come from the documentation range in RFC 5398. Nothing here can
exist or resolve. The technique, the default lockout thresholds and the
authentication error codes, on the other hand, are documented by MITRE and by
Microsoft — see the references at the end.

---

## Context: why the inversion matters

Classic brute force is many passwords against one account. It produces a loud,
easy signature: fail, fail, fail, fail against the same user. The whole defensive
ecosystem is built around that shape.

*Password spraying* swaps the axes: **one password against many accounts.** MITRE
describes it in those words — trying a common password, or a short list, that may
match the domain's complexity policy, against many different accounts, precisely
*to avoid the account lockouts that would normally occur when brute forcing a
single account*.

The consequence is the thing to understand before looking at any logs:

**Failed-attempt lockout is not a spraying detection with a badly chosen threshold.
It is a control that measures something else.** It counts failures inside an
account. An attack that spreads one failure per account leaves it at one, and the
threshold — whether it is ten or three — never gets close.

Microsoft Entra smart lockout illustrates the point with its defaults: it locks
after ten failures in public tenants and after three in US Government tenants, and
it also tracks the last three bad password hashes so that repeating the same wrong
password does not increment the counter. Those are reasonable decisions against
repeated guessing on one account. Against a single attempt per account there is no
threshold that helps: the counter reads one.

Lowering the threshold is not the way out either. MITRE warns about it in
mitigation M1036: too strict a policy creates a denial of service condition and
renders the environment unusable, with every account in the attack locked out. With
spraying, that would be the whole organisation.

## 1. The alert

The ticket that lands in the queue does not say "password spraying". It says this:

```
Alert:           Second factor challenge denied by the user
Severity:        Medium
Account:         p.quiroga@andes-mutual.example
Source:          203.0.113[.]61 - AS64499
Result:          Password correct, second factor denied
Prior attempts on this account (24 h):  1 failed
Assignment group: SOC L1
```

It is a single-account alert with a user action that fully explains it: somebody
tried to get in, the person said no, the access did not happen. The temptation to
close it as "attempt blocked, no impact" is strong, and it is not a stupid
temptation: that access really was blocked.

There is one item in the ticket that does not allow closing it that way. **The
password was correct.** This was not a failed attempt that MFA also stopped: it was
an attempt that reached the second factor because the first one was satisfied.
Somebody, on a network that is not the organisation's, knows that account's
password.

And a password a stranger gets right from the outside is almost never just one
password.

## 3. Changing the axis

The usual triage procedure starts at the entity in the alert and layers context
around it: the account, its owner, its devices, its history. It is a good procedure
for most alerts, and it is the one that does not solve this one.

The query that has to be written does not group by account. It does not group by IP
either, and that part is less obvious: if the attack arrives distributed, grouping
by IP fragments the evidence too. What has to be grouped is **the time window and
the result**, counting distinct accounts.

```kql
SigninLogs
| where TimeGenerated between (ago(24h) .. now())
| where ResultType == 50126                 // invalid username or password
| summarize
    accounts = dcount(UserPrincipalName),
    sources  = dcount(IPAddress),
    attempts = count()
  by bin(TimeGenerated, 1h), AutonomousSystemNumber
| where accounts > 20
| order by TimeGenerated asc
```

The result looks nothing like the ticket:

```
Window    ASN       accounts  sources  attempts
04:00     AS64499         47       11        47
05:00     AS64499         52       14        53
06:00     AS64499         49       12        50
07:00     AS64499         44       13        44
08:00     AS64499         51       14        51
09:00     AS64499         44       12        45
10:00     AS64499          0        0         0

Totals:   287 distinct accounts, 43 source addresses, 290 attempts
Maximum attempts against any single account: 2
```

Two hundred and ninety authentications spread across two hundred and eighty-seven
accounts. The relationship between those two figures is the signature: **a little
over one attempt per account.**

No control that counts inward from an account can see this. The flaw is not in the
threshold, it is in the axis.

## 4. Three details of the pattern

### The source is distributed on purpose

Forty-three addresses for two hundred and ninety attempts: about seven each.
Blocking the IP in the ticket would have stopped under one per cent of the attack,
and the attacker would not even have noticed.

Microsoft documented an even slower version of the same idea: a campaign that used
a single sign-in attempt per account within a twenty-four hour period, precisely to
stay under brute force detection thresholds. Against something like that, the
hourly count that solved this case is not enough either: the window has to widen to
days.

### The error code does not distinguish

The failures return `AADSTS50126`, which means "invalid username or password". It
is deliberately ambiguous: the same code comes back whether the account does not
exist or exists with the wrong password. That ambiguity is a defence against user
enumeration.

So it is worth asking where the list of two hundred and eighty-seven accounts came
from, if the system did not filter it. The answer is that it did not need filtering
from the inside: the organisation publishes addresses in the format
`initial.surname@andes-mutual.example`, and the names of its staff are publicly
available. The list is built outside and tested inside.

Worth writing down as a correction to a common intuition: **an attacker knowing
valid usernames does not imply they enumerated them against the system.**

### The password meets the policy

The password used was `Otoño2026!`. Ten characters, uppercase, lowercase, a number
and a symbol. It passes any complexity policy, and that is exactly why an attacker
tries it: **mandatory complexity pushes a lot of people into the same small set of
passwords** — season plus year plus exclamation mark.

Two people out of two hundred and eighty-seven had it. At that rate, an attacker
aiming at a thousand-account organisation expects seven hits for every password
tried.

## 5. The success nobody saw

The previous query filters on failures. Running it again over successful
authentications, with the same source and the same window, is the step that
uncovers the real incident.

The two rows tell the same story with opposite endings, and that comparison is the
central lesson of the case.

`p.quiroga` had a compromised password just like the other account. There was no
access, and on top of that it **produced the only signal that reached the SOC**. The
second factor did not merely block: it turned a silent compromise into an alert.

`svc-facturacion` is a service account that a management system uses to read a
mailbox over IMAP. That protocol cannot negotiate a second factor, so when the MFA
policy was rolled out the account was added to the exclusion list to keep the system
working. The exclusion is still in place three years after the project that
justified it.

The result is that the most exposed account in the inventory — the one nobody
watches, because there is no person behind it to report anything odd — is also the
only one without the control that stopped the attack everywhere else.

Stated as a working rule: **a spraying attack is not hunting for the weakest
password, it is hunting for the account with the fewest controls.** And the
exclusion list of a conditional access policy is, literally, the inventory of those
accounts, written by the organisation itself.

## 6. What was done from the session

Auditing the mailbox across the five hours and thirty-nine minutes of the session
found mail being read, with no messages sent and no forwarding rule configured.

The billing mailbox receives invoices and payment notices from the mutual's
suppliers. That is reconnaissance material for a transfer fraud: who invoices, for
how much, how often, against which bank account and in what email format. None of
it leaves a trace when it is read.

It is worth being precise about what the evidence supports and what it does not.
What is on record is the access to the content. Fraudulent intent is a reasonable
hypothesis given the mailbox contents and what has been documented in similar
campaigns — not an observed fact.

## 7. Containment

The order answers one question: what is still useful to the attacker right now.

1. **Revoke the `svc-facturacion` sessions.** There is an active session. Until it
   is cut, the access continues even if the secret changes.
2. **Rotate the service account's secret** and disable basic authentication on the
   mailbox, so the protocol that demanded the exclusion stops existing. An exclusion
   without the protocol is a door with no legitimate use.
3. **Reset `p.quiroga`'s password.** There was no access, but the password is
   compromised and still valid.
4. **Add the sprayed password and its variants to the directory's banned password
   list.** Without this, the other two hundred and eighty-five people are free to
   pick it at their next change.
5. **Review the rest of the MFA exclusion list.** Every entry needs a current reason
   or it goes.
6. **Block the source autonomous system**, with low expectations and some care: a
   residential proxy provider shares space with legitimate home connections.

The order is not decorative. Starting at step six, which is what instinct asks for
first, would not have cut the active session.

## 8. What detection gets added

The case leaves one new rule behind, and its value is in the axis it uses:

```kql
SigninLogs
| where ResultType == 50126
| summarize accounts = dcount(UserPrincipalName) by bin(TimeGenerated, 1h)
| where accounts > 15
```

No grouping by account and no grouping by source. The condition is not "many
failures", it is **"many distinct accounts failing"**, which is the shape the attack
has.

Three warnings about that rule, so it does not read as a closed solution:

- The threshold of fifteen accounts per hour has to be calibrated against the
  environment. A Monday morning after a bulk password expiry produces the same
  pattern with no attacker in it.
- Against a campaign of one attempt per account every twenty-four hours, the
  one-hour window sees nothing. A second rule with a multi-day window is needed.
- MITRE notes that an attacker can fall back on LDAP and Kerberos, which are less
  likely to generate high-visibility events such as Windows event 4625, typical of
  failed SMB attempts. A rule over cloud sign-in logs does not cover the equivalent
  in the on-premises directory.

## Lessons learned

**A control that does not fire is not always a control that failed.** The brute
force rule and the lockout did what they define. The mistake would be concluding
from their silence that nothing happened: they do not measure the axis the attack
came down.

**When the entity in the alert does not explain the alert, let go of it.** Triage by
account exhausted the evidence in twenty minutes. Everything else appeared when a
query was written that grouped by neither account nor source.

**The second factor produced the only signal in the incident.** It did not just
block `p.quiroga`'s access: it turned it into a visible event. The account without a
second factor generated none, which is why the compromise ran for five and a half
hours before anyone looked at it.

**A policy's exclusion list is the map of the targets.** It is written by the
organisation, it usually outlives the reason that created it, and it is almost never
reviewed. Worth treating as a risk inventory rather than a configuration detail.

**Mandatory complexity concentrates passwords instead of scattering them.** A
password that satisfies every rule and that two out of every two hundred and
eighty-seven people picked is the predictable result of demanding complexity without
banning the patterns people use to satisfy it.

---

## Recommendations

- Detection based on the count of distinct accounts failing in a window, with a
  second long-window rule for slow campaigns
- Second factor with no exceptions, and where a protocol cannot support it, replace
  the protocol rather than exclude the account
- Periodic review of conditional access exclusions, with expiry dates
- A banned password list carrying local patterns, not just the product's dictionary
- Conditional access blocking authentication from anonymising services and from
  unmanaged devices
- A dedicated alert for successful service account authentication from sources
  outside the declared ranges
