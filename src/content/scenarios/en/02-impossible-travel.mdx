## Executive summary

An *impossible travel* alert lands in the SOC queue. The analyst finds three
reasonable benign explanations — corporate VPN, a trip the user had mentioned, a
known device — and is about to close it as a false positive.

A fourth data point fits none of the three, and turns the case around: the
suspicious session never authenticated. It showed up holding a stolen session
cookie.

This case is about triage, not about malware. The skill it exercises is the
hardest one to teach and the one that lets the most incidents through: **not
closing a ticket on the first plausible explanation.**

**Key metrics:**

- Time to alert: 0 minutes — detection fires automatically at login
- Time to confirm the true positive: 54 minutes
- Time to containment: 78 minutes
- Data compromised: mailbox accessible for 71 minutes, no fraud executed
- Primary technique: T1550.004 (Web Session Cookie)

<Warn />**Synthetic scenario.** The organisation, the user and the logs were built
for the exercise. IP addresses come from the ranges RFC 5737 reserves for
documentation, the domains use the `.example` TLD from RFC 2606, and the autonomous
system numbers come from the documentation range in RFC 5398. Nothing here can
exist or resolve. The techniques and the detection logic, on the other hand, are
documented by MITRE and by Microsoft — see the references at the end.

---

## Context: what this alert detects, and why it fails so often

*Impossible travel* correlates two successful authentications from the same
account and works out the speed you would need to cover the distance between them
in the elapsed time. If that speed is physically impossible, it fires.

It is a cheap and valuable detection, but it comes with a high false positive
rate, and for good reasons: corporate VPNs make a user appear in the city of the
internet egress, proxies rotate between regions, and a phone can jump from mobile
data to corporate Wi-Fi in seconds. Microsoft documents that its implementation
needs between 14 days and 10 sign-ins of history before it produces detections,
and that the model tries on its own to rule out VPNs used by other users in the
directory.

Translated into day-to-day work: **most of these alerts are noise, and that is
exactly what makes them dangerous.** An analyst who closes them on autopilot is
right many times in a row, until they aren't.

## 1. The alert

The ticket arrives at medium severity with a terse description: two successful
sign-ins for `l.ferreyra@northwind-logistics.example`, 95 minutes and one ocean
apart.

The first thing the analyst does is not to investigate the attacker. It is to ask
whether there is one.

## 3. Check the distance before forming an opinion

Before accepting the engine's verdict, it is worth recalculating it. Using the
haversine formula on the published coordinates:

```
Rosario  (-32.9587, -60.6930)
Madrid   ( 40.4168,  -3.7038)

Great-circle distance:   10,037 km
Elapsed time:            95 minutes (1.583 h)
Implied speed:           6,339 km/h  (~Mach 5.2)
```

A long-haul commercial flight cruises at around 900 km/h: it would need 11.2 hours
of pure flight time, before transfers or boarding. Concorde, at 2,180 km/h, took
4.6 hours.

The alert is right about what it claims: **that journey did not happen.** What the
alert does not say — and what has to be worked out — is *why* the two sessions show
up in different places.

---

## 4. The elimination checklist

This is where 90% of these alerts get resolved. Three benign hypotheses, in order
of likelihood.

### Hypothesis 1: the corporate VPN

Northwind's European internet egress is in Madrid. If the user connected to the
VPN, appearing in Madrid is exactly what you would expect.

<Fail />**Doesn't hold.** The Madrid session comes from `AS64500`, a VPS provider.
The corporate range is `198.51.100[.]0/24` on `AS64497`. The city matches; the
infrastructure does not.

This is the most common trap: the geolocation matches and you take the explanation
as settled without checking which network it actually came from.

### Hypothesis 2: a trip

<Fail />**Doesn't hold.** HR has no approved travel on record for that date. And
there is one detail that almost rescues it: two weeks earlier, the user had
mentioned in a team channel that she would be working from Spain for a few days in
April.

It is a plausible, verifiable explanation — for **the wrong month**. A rushed
analyst reads it, finds it coherent, and closes the ticket.

### Hypothesis 3: known device and browser

<Warn />**Matches, but explains nothing.** Both sessions report the same user
agent, `Chrome 133 / Windows 11`.
That could mean it is the same person — or that whoever controls the session
inherited the browser fingerprint along with the cookie. A user agent is a string
the client chooses to send; it authenticates nobody.

---

## 5. The fact that breaks the story

Looking at the authentication events tied to the Madrid session, there are none.

No password validation. No MFA challenge. No error, no retry. The session simply
appears already established, holding a valid token, operating on the mailbox.

And here is what matters: **none of the three benign hypotheses explains that.**

A VPN changes where you connect from; it does not excuse you from authenticating.
Neither does a trip. A known device would still produce its sign-in event. The
absence of authentication is not a minor detail of the alert: it is a different
condition, one that only occurs if somebody presented authentication material that
had already been issued.

That is exactly what technique T1550.004 describes: using a stolen session cookie
to authenticate to a web service, skipping MFA because the session is already
authenticated.

---

## 6. Confirmation

With that lead, the analyst looks at what was done from that session. Two actions
show up in the six minutes after the access.

**09:51 — An MFA device registration.** A new second factor is enrolled on the
account. The stolen cookie has an expiry date; an enrolled device outlives the
session and even survives a badly executed password change. That is persistence
(T1098.005).

**09:53 — An inbox rule.** The `RSS-Sync` rule moves out of sight every incoming
message containing "invoice", "transfer" or "bank".

That rule is what closes the case, and it is worth understanding what it reveals.
It is not there to steal information: it is there so the user **does not see** the
replies to the messages the attacker plans to send from her account. It is the
groundwork for email fraud, and it is a documented pattern in campaigns that pair
session theft with business email compromise (T1564.008).

The case stops being a geolocation alert and becomes an identity compromise in
progress.

---

## 7. Containment

The order matters. Resetting the password first is the classic mistake: it does not
invalidate active sessions, does not touch the enrolled device, and tells the
attacker they have been spotted.

1. **Revoke every session token** on the account. Without this, the stolen cookie
   stays valid even after a password change.
2. **Remove the device enrolled** as a second factor.
3. **Reset credentials** and force re-enrolment of legitimate MFA.
4. **Delete the inbox rule** and check whether there are others.
5. **Audit the mailbox**: what was read, what was sent, what was downloaded.

In this case the audit found no mail sent from the account. The attacker was
building the scaffolding for the fraud, not running it yet.

## Lessons learned

**A plausible explanation is not a verification.** All three benign hypotheses were
reasonable and two of them nearly fitted. The difference between closing well and
closing badly was asking for the piece of data that confirmed each hypothesis,
instead of accepting that it sounded coherent.

**Look for what none of the hypotheses explains.** What solved the case was not
finding more evidence, but noticing that one fact — the session with no
authentication — did not fit into any of the benign explanations. That is the
thread to pull.

**MFA was not defeated, it was bypassed.** No second factor was broken. The
attacker walked in with an already-authenticated session, where MFA had already
happened. It is a distinction that completely changes which control needs
strengthening.

**The actions after the access are what confirm it.** The access itself was
ambiguous. The enrolled device and the inbox rule are not: nobody does that by
accident.

**The order of containment matters.** Changing the password without revoking
sessions leaves the stolen cookie working and tips off the attacker.

---

## Recommendations

- <Pass />Bind the session to the device, so a token issued on a managed machine is
  useless from another one
- <Pass />MFA that resists relaying, based on origin-bound passkeys rather than
  one-time codes
- <Pass />Alert on second-factor device registrations outside employee onboarding
- <Pass />Alert on inbox rules that filter on financial or security-related words
- <Pass />Add to the impossible travel procedure an explicit check of the ASN and of
  the presence of authentication events, not just of the geolocation
