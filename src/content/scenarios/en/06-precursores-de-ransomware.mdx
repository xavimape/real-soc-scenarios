## Executive summary

At 02:08 on 7 March, six servers and one hundred and eighteen workstations were
encrypted.

The SOC had received three alerts before that, spread across three days and change.
All three were closed. **And all three were closed defensibly**, because each one
described something the company does routinely: installing a remote access tool,
sending data to a cloud storage provider, running administrative tasks against
several machines.

The one alert that admitted no other reading arrived at 02:06 on 7 March: the
deletion of the volume shadow copies. One hundred and eighteen seconds before the
encryption.

That gap is the subject of the case, and it is not bad luck. **The unambiguous
signals of a ransomware attack belong to the impact phase, which is the end.
Everything that arrives in time is ambiguous by definition.** The skill this case
exercises is not recognising a malicious indicator: it is disambiguating legitimate
activity.

**Key metrics:**

- Alerts raised before the encryption: 3, all closed as expected activity
- Time from the first alert to the encryption: 3 days, 11 hours and 46 minutes
- Time between the only unambiguous alert and the encryption: 118 seconds
- Attacker tools that were not malicious: all of them
- Malware samples detected before the encryption: none
- Primary techniques: T1219.002, T1021.002, T1567.002, T1490

<Warn />**Synthetic scenario.** The organisation, the machines and the alerts were
built for the exercise. IP addresses come from the ranges RFC 5737 reserves for
documentation and the domains use the `.example` TLD from RFC 2606. The techniques,
the commands and the pattern of abusing legitimate tools are documented by MITRE and
in CISA advisories — see the references at the end.

<Warn />**What this case does not cover.** The scenario starts with the operator
already inside the environment. How initial access was obtained is the subject of
cases 01 and 03; what is exercised here is everything that happens afterwards, which
is where the SOC had its opportunities and had them poorly served.

## Context: the organisation

Ribera Alimentos distributes refrigerated food. Four hundred and fifty workstations,
twenty-two servers, an IT team of four and no SOC of its own: the EDR is a managed
service and its alerts land in a queue handled by a first-line analyst, among other
duties.

It is an ordinary profile, and it is worth keeping in mind when judging the decisions
that follow. There is no negligence in this case. There is a procedure that allowed
closing a ticket without an authoritative answer, and three chances to discover it.

Read like that, one at a time, all three are reasonable. None of them contains a
malicious file, a bad-reputation domain or an address from an unexpected country. The
attacker brought nothing: **they used what was already there, or what the company
already allowed.**

It is what CISA advisories describe for several active ransomware families. The
groups that operate with commercial remote access tools do so precisely because the
software is legitimate, signed and, in many organisations, approved.

## 2. What each alert was missing

Here is the method of the case. None of the three alerts can be resolved with what
the alert carries. **That is not a limitation of the analyst: it is the finding.** And
each one has a specific question, aimed at a specific person, that would have
resolved it in minutes.

| Alert | What it said | What was missing | Who to ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | An approved tool was installed | Who installed it, under which account, and whether a change was authorised | Change management |
| 2 | Data went to an allowed destination | What the normal volume is from that server to that destination | Infrastructure |
| 3 | Administrative tasks were executed | Whether a maintenance window was recorded that night | IT lead |

And here is what each answer would have revealed:

**Alert 1.** The tool is approved **on the machines IT administers, installed by the
deployment account, during business hours, against a request.** This installation was
on a finance administration workstation, under the user's local account, with no
request behind it. Policy approved the software; it did not approve that
installation.

**Alert 2.** The nightly backup on `SRV-ARCH-02` runs at 01:00 and moves about
2.8 GB. This transfer started at 03:14 and moved 42 GB. Same destination, same
protocol, fifteen times the volume and three hours outside the window. **The allowed
destination explained where the data was going, not why there was so much of it.**

**Alert 3.** The change record held no maintenance window for the night of 6 March.
The phrase "could be, we were doing updates" was a busy person's impression, not a
fact. **The change system had the authoritative answer and nobody consulted it.**

Three questions. None requires a tool the company did not have, or a skill the
analyst did not have. What was missing was a procedure that did not allow closing
without an answer.

## 3. The rule that comes out of this

Worth stating in a way that is useful on Monday:

**The output of triaging an ambiguous alert is not "true positive" or "false
positive". It is a question aimed at whoever holds the authoritative answer.**

A ticket closed with "expected activity" and no source behind it is not resolved: it
is filed. The difference between those two things is the only thing separating this
case from one where nothing happens.

And the uncomfortable corollary, worth making explicit because it protects the
analyst: **closing alert 1 was statistically correct.** Eleven out of eleven identical
alerts the previous month had been legitimate. At that base rate, closing is the bet
that wins nearly every time. The problem was not the person's judgement, it was that
the system asked them to guess instead of giving them something to verify with.

Fixing this by asking the analyst to "be more suspicious" does not work and makes
things worse: it raises the cost of every ticket and changes none of the available
information. What works is the alert arriving with the context attached — installing
account, associated change ticket, host baseline — or the procedure forcing someone to
go and fetch it.

## 4. The correlation nobody made

The three alerts are in the same console. In the console they are three rows
separated by days, from three different rules, with three different severities.
Nothing joins them.

But they share entities:

```
ADM-FIN-07 ─── alert 1 (3 Mar)  remote access installed
     │
     └───────── alert 3 (6 Mar)  remote execution against 6 servers

ADM-FIN-07 account ─── authentication to SRV-ARCH-02 (4 Mar 02:55)
                            │
                            └── alert 2 (4 Mar)  42 GB outbound
```

The same machine appears in the first and the third. The account operating that
machine appears authenticating against the server from the second, nineteen minutes
before the transfer begins.

**The unit of analysis is not the alert, it is the entity.** Three low-severity alerts
on the same host in four days are a different fact from three low-severity alerts on
three arbitrary hosts, and no console sorted by time and severity shows that
difference.

That translates into a concrete tooling request: being able to see every alert for a
host or an account in one view, whatever rule produced it. Where that does not exist,
it is covered by a step in the procedure: **before closing, look up the entity in the
history.** It is slower and it works just the same.

## 5. The final two minutes

At 02:06 on 7 March the following ran on six servers simultaneously:

```
vssadmin.exe delete shadows /all /quiet
```

MITRE documents that command verbatim under T1490, alongside `wbadmin.exe delete
catalog -quiet` and the `bcdedit.exe` modifications that disable Windows automatic
recovery. It is one of the least ambiguous signals there is: **the bulk, silent
deletion of shadow copies has no reasonable administrative reading**, least of all on
six servers at once at two in the morning.

The alert was raised at high severity. Nobody saw it: at 02:08 the encryption began,
and at that hour the queue had nobody in it.

Worth drawing the right conclusion here, because there is a tempting wrong one. The
wrong one is "night shift coverage was needed". With two minutes, a night shift does
not make it either: while the analyst is opening the ticket, encryption is already
under way.

The right one is that **a detection that only fires in the impact phase is not a
detection, it is a notification.** It is good for knowing what happened, not for
preventing it. Its value is forensic. The preventive value was three days earlier,
spread across three tickets that were closed well.

That said: shadow copy deletion deserves an automated response, not an alert.
Isolating the host first and asking afterwards is the only speed that beats that
command.

## 6. Containment and recovery

What was done, in order, and why.

1. **Isolate the affected machines from the network**, cutting the route to the
   backups as well. The first concern after encryption is that it does not reach what
   is still healthy.
2. **Disable the compromised account and revoke its sessions**, including the remote
   access tool installed on `ADM-FIN-07`.
3. **Hunt for that same tool across the rest of the estate.** It turned up on two more
   workstations, installed on 5 March, with no alert associated because the rule had
   already been muted for volume.
4. **Verify the state of the offline backups** before touching anything else.
5. **Only then restore**, prioritising by dependency rather than by declared
   importance.

Step three is the one most often skipped and the one that matters most: after an
encryption, the question is not only what broke, but **where else the attacker's
access is**. Restoring onto a network where a door is still open is repeating the
incident with more work.

And there is a consequence the encryption obscures: **the 42 GB left three days
earlier.** Even if the restoration were perfect and everything came back in hours,
that data is outside. The encryption incident is resolved with backups; the
exfiltration one is not resolved.

|---|---|---|
| **T1219.002** | Remote Access Tools: Remote Desktop Software | The commercial tool installed on ADM-FIN-07 on 3 March |
| **T1021.002** | Remote Services: SMB/Windows Admin Shares | The execution against six servers on 6 March |

| **T1486** | Data Encrypted for Impact | The encryption at 02:08 |

Naming note: **T1219 was renamed *Remote Access Tools*** and now has sub-techniques;
abusing a remote desktop tool maps to `T1219.002`. In older material the same
technique appears as *Remote Access Software*, with no sub-techniques, under the same
`T1219` identifier. If a rule or an old report says the latter, it is talking about
the same thing.

Worth noticing how that table splits. The first four rows are the tactics where the
SOC had time; the last is where it did not. And **none of the five techniques required
malicious software**: four use legitimate tools or operating system components, and
only the last is an attacker binary.

## Lessons learned

**What arrives in time is ambiguous; what is unambiguous arrives late.** It is not bad
luck, it is the shape of the intrusion: the signals with no second reading live in the
impact phase. A SOC that only acts on the unambiguous always acts afterwards.

**The output of triaging an ambiguous alert is a question, not a verdict.** Aimed at
whoever holds the authoritative answer: change management, infrastructure, the owner
of the host. Closing without that answer is filing, not resolving.

**A tool being approved does not approve every installation.** Policy approved the
software; it did not approve a finance user's account installing it on a Tuesday
afternoon with no request. The right question is not what was installed but who, where
and against what authorisation.

**An allowed destination explains the direction, not the volume.** The 42 GB went to
the same provider the backups use. Without a baseline for the server, the alert had
nothing to compare against and there was nothing to decide.

**The unit of analysis is the entity, not the alert.** Three low-severity alerts on the
same host in four days are a different fact from three loose alerts, and a console
sorted by time does not show the difference.

**The analyst who closed the first alert was right by the base rate.** Eleven out of
eleven identical cases had been legitimate. Demanding more suspicion fixes nothing:
what has to change is the information that arrives with the alert.

**Encryption and exfiltration are two incidents.** Backups resolve one. The other
already happened three days earlier and there is no going back.

## Recommendations

- Enrich software installation alerts with the account that performed the install and
  the associated change ticket, so verification does not depend on somebody going to
  look for it
- Baseline outbound volume per server and per destination, and alert on deviation from
  that baseline rather than on a fixed threshold
- An entity view in the console: every alert for a host or an account, whatever the
  rule. Without it, a mandatory history lookup step before closing
- Forbid closing a ticket as "expected activity" without recording the source that
  confirms it
- Automated isolation in response to shadow copy deletion or bulk execution of
  `vssadmin`, `wbadmin` or `bcdedit`, without waiting for an analyst
- Application control limiting remote access tools to IT machines, rather than
  approving them across the whole estate
- Review which rules are muted for volume and why: a noisy rule that gets switched off
  stops existing as a detection
- Offline backups, verified with test restorations rather than with the backup job's
  status
