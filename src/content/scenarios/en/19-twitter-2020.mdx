## Executive summary

On 15 July 2020, a seventeen-year-old and his accomplices took control of dozens of
accounts on the social platform most used to spread public information, and posted a
cryptocurrency scam from them.

The regulator's report is explicit about the method: **there was no malware, no
exploits and no backdoors.** There were phone calls. Someone rang employees claiming to
be from the IT department, offered help with a remote access problem that was real and
frequent, and walked them to a site identical to the real one.

For a SOC there is one fact that organises the whole case and tends to get overlooked:
**several employees reported the suspicious calls that same morning**, and the response
team was investigating those reports when, at 15:18, the public account takeovers
began.

The signal existed, it arrived in time and it arrived through the right channel: people.
**The question in this case is not how to detect it, it is what you do with a report
like that in the hours between suspicion and impact.**

**Key metrics:**

- Total duration of the incident: about 24 hours
- Accounts compromised: 130
- Accounts used to post: 45
- Accounts whose data was downloaded with the internal tool: 7
- Amount stolen in cryptocurrency: about 118,000 dollars
- Time without a chief information security officer: seven months
- Main techniques: T1598.004, T1566.002, T1621, T1078, T1552.001

<Warn />**Real case.** The facts, times and figures come from the investigation report
by the New York State Department of Financial Services, published in October 2020. It
is a document by a regulator with investigative powers, not a journalistic
reconstruction.

## Context: why the pretext worked

The pretext was a problem with remote access. It is worth understanding why it was
credible, because that is where the transferable lesson sits.

In March 2020 the organisation moved to fully remote work, like almost everyone. That
change pushed the infrastructure to its limits and **connection problems were
frequent.** Everybody had them and everybody talked about them.

```
Invented pretext:       "There's a problem with your VPN"
Employee's reality:      They did have problems with the VPN
Result:                  The pretext did not need believing, it was already lived
```

A good pretext does not convince: **it confirms something the person already believes.**
And in 2020 that sentence described the daily experience of millions of people.

The attackers also used personal information about each employee to sound legitimate.
The report puts something on the record that is worth stating: it found no evidence
that any employee knowingly helped.

## 1. The second factor did not fail: it was handed over

This is the technical point of the case and it is worth taking apart slowly, because it
contradicts a very widespread belief.

The scheme went like this:

```
1. The person enters the fake site and types username and password
2. The attacker types them into the real site at the same time
3. The real site asks the person for the second factor
4. The person, believing they are signing in, approves it
5. The attacker is in
```

<Fail />The second factor worked exactly as designed: it asked the account holder for
confirmation and the account holder confirmed.

<Pass />What that design lacks is for the confirmation to be bound to the site being
entered, not only to the person.

**A second factor a person can transcribe or approve is one more secret, with a shorter
shelf life.** Factors resistant to this attack — the ones that cryptographically verify
the domain before responding — would not have worked on the fake site, because the
domain does not match and the factor itself refuses.

For an analyst this changes a habitual question. Faced with a suspicious access, "did it
have a second factor?" is not enough. The useful question is **what kind it was**, and
whether the user had to decide anything.

---

## 2. The escalation was reading the intranet

The first compromised account was useless for the objective: it had no access to the
internal account administration tools.

What they did with it is what anyone would do: **browse the internal sites and read the
documentation.** The intranet pages explained how to access other internal applications
and, with that, who used them. Armed with that map, on 15 July they called employees who
did have the access they were after.

This is worth pausing on, because it describes an asset that almost never appears in a
risk inventory: **internal documentation is reconnaissance material.** It is not secret,
it is not classified, and it does not need to be. But to someone already inside with any
account, it is the map that turns a useless access into a valuable one.

From that follows a concrete, cheap detection: an account reading internal
documentation about systems it does not use, and staff directories for teams it does not
work with, is an observable anomaly. It proves nothing on its own, but it is exactly the
kind of signal that adds weight to others.

## 3. The window the SOC had, and what to do with it

This is the heart of the case for anyone working a shift.

**Several employees reported the calls and suspicious sign-ins on the morning of the
15th.** The response team was investigating those reports when, at 15:18, the public
part began.

Hours passed between the two. The question is not whether it was detected — it was —
but what can be done in those hours with incomplete information.

next call into a detector, and it costs nothing if it turns out to be a false positive.

## 4. Containment does damage too

Containment ended up preventing many verified accounts from posting and locking those
that had changed their password in the previous thirty days.

The report records the effect: **the national weather service could not post a tornado
advisory**, and even the regulator's own account was out of service for several hours.

I do not bring this up to criticise the decision. I bring it up because **it is the only
honest way to talk about containment.** Stopping an incident in progress on a public
platform means switching off functions other people need, and someone has to decide that
with incomplete information and the clock running.

What a SOC can do in advance is prepare that decision:

- **Know what gets switched off and whom it affects**, before needing to
- **Have it written down who authorises** each high-impact measure, with a name and a
  deputy
- **Separate the reversible from the irreversible.** Revoking sessions can be undone;
  deleting cannot

Internally the measure was just as drastic: employee access to internal systems was
restricted, with long delays in serving users, and every person had to change their
password on a video call in front of their supervisor. That is the right response when
you do not know how many accounts are compromised: **you assume any of them could be.**

## 5. The ones who did cut the chain

There is a part of the case that gets told less often and is the most encouraging.

The regulated cryptocurrency companies reacted fast and blocked transfers to the
published addresses. The report gives the figures:

| Company | Transfers blocked | Approximate value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A large exchange | about 5,670 | 1,294,000 dollars |
| A payments company | 358 | 51,000 dollars |
| Another exchange | 2 | 1,800 dollars |
| One more | 1 | 250 dollars |

Actual customer losses at those companies came to about 22,000 dollars: **1.63 per cent
of what was blocked in time.**

The lesson is direct. The incident happened at one organisation, and the damage was
contained at others that were not compromised and that acted on a public indicator — a
wallet address — within minutes. **Defending is not always defending your own.**

## Lessons learned

**The best pretext is the one that is already true.** Nobody had to invent a problem:
remote access really did fail, every day. Any known friction in the organisation is
social engineering material.

**A second factor the person approves can be handed over.** The question is not whether
there is a second factor, but whether the factor verifies the site or leaves that
verification to the user.

**Internal documentation is reconnaissance.** It does not need hiding, but it is worth
knowing that it turns a worthless access into a valuable one, and that reading it from
an account that has no business there is an observable signal.

**A person's report arrived before any tool.** And it arrived with hours to spare. What
decides the outcome is not the detection, it is what gets done with it while the
information is still incomplete.

**Containment has a cost and it has to have been thought through in advance.** When
containment prevents a tornado advisory from going out, the discussion about who
authorises what has already arrived late.

**An uncompromised third party can cut the impact.** Blocking a published wallet address
prevented more than a million dollars in transfers.

---

## Recommendations

**A reporting channel people actually use, and that does not punish them.** The whole
case rests on employees who picked up the phone to warn. If reporting is cumbersome or
exposes the reporter, that signal does not exist.

**A second factor resistant to site impersonation** for remote access and for internal
administration tools. It is the only control that would have cut this chain at the
fourth link.

**A clear, repeated rule: IT never asks for credentials by phone.** And a known internal
number to call back on when someone tries.

**Access to account administration tools by exception and with logging.** How many
people can operate on someone else's account, and whether that number is justified.

**Two-person verification for irreversible actions** on other people's accounts.

**A containment plan with declared impact.** What gets switched off, whom it affects, who
authorises it and what is reversible. Written beforehand, not during.
