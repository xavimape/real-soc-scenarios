## Executive summary

On 19 March 2019, the LockerGoga ransomware encrypted the systems of Norsk Hydro, an
aluminium producer operating in forty countries. All 35,000 employees were affected.
Most of its manufacturing plants moved to manual operations.

The eight previous cases on this site are about detecting in time. This one starts
where those end badly: **the encryption already happened, containment stopped existing
as a category, and all that lies ahead are decisions under pressure.**

It is here for three specific reasons, and none of them is the malware.

**The first:** LockerGoga changes account passwords and logs users out of their
sessions. That is, **it took the keys away from the team that had to respond.** A
response plan that assumes you will be able to log in is a plan that does not work
here.

**The second:** it did not spread on its own. MITRE records that it moved across the
network by being copied by hand from machine to machine, not as a worm. There were
people on the other side, operating live, while the response team worked.

**The third:** the company made three decisions on day one — pay no ransom, bring in
outside help, communicate openly — and sustained the third with open broadcasts while
the incident was under way. That transparency was not a communications virtue: it was
a defensive decision, and it is worth understanding why it worked.

**Key metrics:**

- Date of encryption: 19 March 2019
- People affected inside the organisation: 35,000, across 40 countries
- Plants that moved to manual operations: most of around 160
- Ransom paid: none
- Decisions made on day one: three
- Primary techniques: T1486, T1531, T1570, plus three techniques from the ICS matrix

<Warn />**Real case.** The technical material comes from the LockerGoga entry in MITRE
ATT&CK (S0372), which in turn cites analysis from several research teams and Hydro's
own incident page. The description of the response comes from the Microsoft security
team publication; Microsoft took part in the recovery.

<Warn />**On the financial figures.** Estimates of around seventy-five million dollars
circulated. Hydro published its own impact in quarterly reports, in Norwegian kroner. I
did not verify those figures against the original reports, so I mention them as an
order of magnitude and not as data.

## Context: why a recovery case is studied differently

In the eight previous cases, the question that organises the analysis is always the
same: what signal was there, when, and why was it not acted on. That is the right
question while something can still be prevented.

After the encryption that question remains valid for the post-incident report, and is
**useless for the next seventy-two hours**. What replaces triage is a different list:

- What is broken and what is not yet
- What has to be switched off before it breaks too
- In what order things get switched back on
- Who communicates, to whom, and how often
- What people work with in the meantime

None of the five is a purely technical question. All five are answered better if they
were answered earlier, in the cold, when nobody was shouting.

And there is a fundamental difference from everything before: **here the enemy is not
uncertainty about what happened, it is time.** What happened is perfectly well known.
The problem is that every hour has a cost and the decisions cannot wait.

## 1. The malware that takes the keys from whoever responds

Of everything LockerGoga does, this is the characteristic a response team needs to
know before meeting it.

MITRE records it under technique **T1531, Account Access Removal**: LockerGoga was
observed **changing account passwords and logging off current users**.

Consider what that means at three in the morning.

The on-call analyst gets the notification. They go to their machine to connect to the
console, and their own session is already closed and their password is no longer their
password. The administration console is on an encrypted server. The corporate
directory, which is where credentials would be reset, is affected. The ticketing
system too. Internal email too.

**The incident destroyed the tools with which the incident is answered.**

From this comes the most concrete requirement the case leaves behind, and it is one
almost no written plan covers:

> Every response plan needs at least one access path that does not depend on the
> infrastructure the incident can take down. Emergency credentials held offline, a
> communication channel that is not the corporate one, and a copy of the plan itself on
> paper or on a separate device.

And the corresponding verification, which is the part that gets skipped: **that path
has to be tested.** An envelope of emergency credentials nobody has opened in three
years has a high chance of holding a password that no longer exists.

---

## 2. It was not a worm

The second characteristic that changes the response.

MITRE describes it under **T1570, Lateral Tool Transfer**: LockerGoga moved across the
victim network **over SMB, and analysis indicates the actors were copying the files
from computer to computer manually rather than self-propagating**.

The difference between a worm and a hands-on operation is enormous for whoever
responds.

A worm is a program: it behaves the same way every time, it does not learn, and once
you understand its logic you know what it will do. A manual operation has **people on
the other side**, looking at the same network, able to notice that a segment is being
isolated and to speed up before the isolation finishes.

That changes two things in the response playbook.

**Isolation is done completely or not at all.** Cutting piecemeal warns the operator
and gives them time. It is the same logic as the containment order in case 02: the
partial action notifies the adversary without taking away their capability.

**The response's own communications move off the compromised network.** If the
corporate directory is in someone else's hands, internal chat and internal email are a
channel shared with the attacker. Coordination moves to personal phones, to a group
outside the company's infrastructure, or to a physical room.

---

## 3. The three decisions of day one

According to the Microsoft security team publication — Microsoft took part in the
recovery — leadership made three swift decisions: **pay no ransom, summon outside
help, and communicate openly.**

Each is worth looking at, because all three are executive decisions and all three have
immediate technical consequences.

**Not paying** defines the whole recovery plan. With no decryption key, the only source
of truth is the backups, and the entire effort orients towards restoration and
rebuilding rather than negotiation. It is a decision to take early because the two
routes diverge immediately.

**Summoning outside help** on day one, not in week two. In an incident of this scale
the problem is not a lack of internal talent: it is that the internal team is also
without access, without email, and thirty-six hours into a shift. External help brings
capacity and, above all, rested people.

**Communicating openly** is the one that most looks like public relations and least is.
I develop it separately.

## 4. Why transparency was a defensive decision

Publicly saying you are encrypted, while you are encrypted, is counter-intuitive. The
reasons it worked are operational:

**It takes the weapon away from the extortion.** A good part of the pressure in an
attack like this is the threat of revealing what happened. Announcing it first turns
that threat into nothing.

**It replaces the internal channel that does not exist.** With corporate email down,
35,000 people need to find out something from somewhere. Public, frequent communication
reaches employees by the same route as everybody else, and stops rumour filling the
vacuum.

**It orders the outside.** Customers, suppliers and the market are going to ask anyway.
A single periodic source costs far less than answering each enquiry separately, and it
sustains the credibility of everything else that is said.

**It protects the technical team internally.** When the whole organisation knows what
is happening and what is being done, the pressure on the people doing the recovery
drops and tolerance for the recovery taking as long as it takes goes up.

Now the part that has to be said for the lesson to be usable rather than a moral:
**this is decided beforehand.** Communicating well in a crisis requires having agreed in
advance who speaks, how often, what can and cannot be said, and coordination with
legal. Improvised at three in the morning, it goes badly.

---

## 5. Going back to paper

MITRE records, citing Hydro's own incident page, that **most of its roughly 160
manufacturing plants moved to manual operations**, with no information systems in the
loop.

That deserves a careful reading, because it is the most useful finding in the case and
the easiest to romanticise.

Going back to paper is not folklore or heroic improvisation. **It is a continuity plan
that existed, or that was possible because there were still people who knew how to
operate without the systems.** In a plant with operators of twenty years' experience,
that capability is available. In a process designed to be inoperable without software,
it is not.

From this comes an uncomfortable question worth asking in the cold, and it is not a
question about information security but about design:

> What part of our operation can keep running, and for how long, without any of the
> systems that assist it today? And is there anybody left who still knows how?

And the one that comes with it, more concrete still: **is the manual procedure written
down somewhere that is not the system that goes down?** An emergency instruction stored
on the corporate intranet is an instruction you will not be able to read exactly when
you need it.

---

## 6. The order of restoration

With the decision not to pay taken, recovery is a sequencing problem. Three criteria,
in this order:

**First, a clean environment to restore into.** Restoring onto the compromised network
reinfects what was restored and, worse, hands the operator back the access that had
just been taken away. This is what makes recovery from an incident of this scale
measured in weeks rather than days: the place has to be built before moving in.

**Then, dependencies, not importance.** The system management declares most critical
usually needs three less glamorous services to start: directory, name resolution,
authentication, time. Restoring by declared importance produces systems that are
switched on and do not work. The order is dictated by the dependency graph, and that
graph has to be drawn beforehand, because in the middle of a crisis nobody reconstructs
it from memory.

**Finally, verification before reconnecting.** Each restored system is validated before
returning to the general network. Haste here is what produces the second wave.

And a warning about backups that this case leaves implicit: **the status of the backup
job is not the same as the existence of a restorable backup.** The only verification
that counts is having restored a test. It is the same recommendation that closes case
06, and here you can see why: when the decision not to pay depends on those backups,
the restoration test stops being good practice and becomes the condition that makes the
decision available.

## 7. Production stopped without touching a controller

ATT&CK has a separate matrix for industrial control environments, and MITRE maps three
of its techniques to this incident, citing Hydro's own page:

| MITRE ICS | Technique | In this case |
|---|---|---|
| **T0829** | Loss of View | The systems used to supervise the process became unavailable |
| **T0827** | Loss of Control | The loss of control forced the switch to manual operations |
| **T0828** | Loss of Productivity and Revenue | Most of around 160 plants operating by hand |

The reading that mapping enables is the one that matters for a SOC, and it is worth
stating carefully:

**Impact on production does not require the malware to reach the controller.**

A plant does not stop only because somebody manipulates a controller. It also stops
when the IT layer from which it is planned, supervised, recorded and dispatched
disappears. *Loss of view* is exactly that: the physical process may keep running, and
nobody can see it.

For an analyst working in an industrial organisation, the practical consequence is that
**the boundary between IT and OT protects less than the diagram suggests.** The diagram
shows two zones separated by a firewall. Operations show that one of those zones needs
the other to function, and that dependency is not drawn anywhere.

It is also worth noting that this is the first case on this site to use the ICS matrix.
That is not a catalogue detail: if you work in industry, energy, water or transport,
**the mapping that matters to your organisation is probably not in the enterprise
matrix** but in this other one, where the techniques do not describe what the attacker
does but what the organisation loses.

### Breakdown

| MITRE ID | Technique | In this case |
|---|---|---|
| **T1570** | Lateral Tool Transfer | Manual copying of the binary over SMB, machine by machine |
| **T1553.002** | Subvert Trust Controls: Code Signing | Signed with stolen certificates, to look more legitimate |
| **T1685** | Disable or Modify Tools | A process-kill command immediately before, to disable the antivirus |
| **T1070.004** | Indicator Removal: File Deletion | Deletes its own launcher after execution |
| **T1486** | Data Encrypted for Impact | Encryption with RSA-OAEP MGF1, including operating system files |
| **T1531** | Account Access Removal | Password changes and closure of active sessions |
| **T1529** | System Shutdown/Reboot | Shutting down infected systems |

Two notes on that table.

**On T1486.** MITRE records that LockerGoga also encrypts operating system files. That
is worse than it sounds for recovery: a machine whose operating system is encrypted
does not boot, so there is no in-place decryption possible even with a key. The only
way out is reinstall and restore.

**On the naming of T1685.** The LockerGoga entry today maps disabling the antivirus to
`T1685, Disable or Modify Tools`, as a top-level technique. In older material you will
find the same behaviour described under *Impair Defenses*. It is the same behaviour
reorganised, and it is the second case on this site where it is worth checking the date
of a reference before comparing identifiers — the other is T1219 in case 06.

## Lessons learned

**The incident can destroy the tools with which it is answered.** LockerGoga changed
passwords and closed sessions. A plan that assumes you will be able to log in, read
internal email and open the console does not describe this scenario. An independent
access path is needed, and it needs to have been tested.

**A worm and a hands-on operation are not the same.** With people on the other side,
partial isolation warns and does not protect. And the response's communications move
off the compromised network, because the attacker is on it.

**Not paying is a decision bought in advance.** It is only available if there are
offline backups somebody tested by restoring. Without that it is not a brave decision,
it is a decision that does not exist.

**The transparency was defensive.** It took the weapon from the extortion, replaced the
downed internal channel, ordered the external conversation and reduced pressure on the
technical team. And it worked because it was prepared: who speaks, how often, and what
can be said.

**The continuity plan has to be executable without the systems that go down.**
Including the plan itself: an instruction stored on the intranet cannot be read when
the intranet is not there.

**Restoration is ordered by dependencies, and into a clean environment.** The dependency
graph has to be drawn beforehand; in the middle of a crisis nobody reconstructs it from
memory.

**Production stops without anybody touching a controller.** *Loss of view* and *loss of
control* describe exactly that, and they are ICS matrix techniques. The IT/OT boundary
protects less than the diagram suggests, because the diagram does not draw the
dependency.

---

## Recommendations

- Offline emergency credentials, with a procedure for their use, and a periodic test
  confirming they still work
- A crisis communication channel independent of the corporate infrastructure, agreed
  and practised
- A copy of the response plan on a medium that does not depend on the systems the plan
  covers
- Periodic restoration testing, with a record: the only evidence a backup exists is
  having restored it
- A maintained dependency graph between systems, as input to the restoration order
- Manual operating procedures written down, accessible offline, and staff trained to
  execute them
- A crisis communication plan approved in advance, with defined spokespeople and legal
  coordination
- For industrial environments: map which OT functions depend on IT services, and treat
  that dependency as impact surface
- Isolation designed to be executed completely and quickly, not in stages
