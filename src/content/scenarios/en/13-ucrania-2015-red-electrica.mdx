## Executive summary

On 23 December 2015, three Ukrainian electricity distribution companies lost control of
their grid. Breakers were opened at around thirty substations and some 225,000 people
were left without power for close to six hours.

It is the first power outage caused by a cyber attack to be documented in detail, and
the joint E-ISAC and SANS report is probably the best public material that exists on an
operational technology incident.

It is here for three things, and none of them is the malware.

**The first.** No program opened the breakers. MITRE records it under technique
`T0823`: the attackers **used the SCADA system's own graphical interfaces** to open
them. And `T0886` adds the detail that makes the case unforgettable: they used **IT
help desk software to move the mouse** on the control machines. Operators watched the
cursor move across their own screens and could not stop it.

**The second.** They did not only attack the process: **they attacked the recovery**.
They overwrote the firmware of the serial-to-Ethernet converters so no command could be
sent remotely. They wiped workstations. They scheduled the uninterruptible power
supplies to shut down the servers. And they flooded the customer call centre so
customers could not even report the outage.

**The third.** Everything a SOC could have detected happened **months earlier and on
the corporate network**, not the industrial one.

**Key metrics:**

- Date: 23 December 2015
- People without supply: around 225,000
- Substations with breakers opened: about 30
- Duration of the outage: close to six hours
- Restoration: largely **manual**
- Malware that operated the breakers: none
- Primary techniques: T1566.001 and T1078 in the enterprise matrix, T0823 and T0886 in ICS

<Warn />**Real case.** The technical material comes from the joint E-ISAC and SANS ICS
report of 18 March 2016, and from campaign `C0028` in MITRE ATT&CK, which attributes it
to Sandworm Team (`G0034`). Figures for duration and reach vary somewhat between
sources and between affected companies; I use the ones MITRE records.

## Context: what changes when the target is a physical process

The twelve previous cases on this site are about data: stealing it, encrypting it,
exposing it. This one is about **electricity**, and that difference reorders everything.

**The impact is not informational, it is physical.** Nobody lost a file. Homes, traffic
lights, boilers and water pumps went dark, in December, in Ukraine.

**No backup restores this.** A backup returns data. It does not return a breaker to its
position: a person does that, at the substation.

**And there is a striking asymmetry of time**, worth keeping in mind while reading the
rest: the campaign had been on the corporate network for months, and the impact lasted
six hours.

One clarification is also worth making for anyone who does not work in industry, because
this case gets cited a lot and understood little. **There was no industrial malware.** No
controller was exploited, no automation logic was modified, and no deep knowledge of
industrial protocols was required for the moment of the outage. What was required was
reaching the operator's computer and using it.

## 1. People opened the breakers

This is worth pausing on because it corrects the most widely circulated version of the
case.

MITRE records two ICS matrix techniques that describe the moment of the outage:

**`T0823`, Graphical User Interface.** Sandworm Team used the operator workstations'
graphical interfaces in the SCADA environment to open the breakers.

**`T0886`, Remote Services.** They used IT help desk software **to move the mouse** on
the control machines, and thereby release the breakers.

In other words: the same kind of tool a support technician uses to connect to a
colleague's computer and help them. The same class of program that appears in case 006
being installed on a finance workstation.

The consequences for defence are concrete and uncomfortable:

**No signature detects this.** The SCADA application did exactly what it knows how to
do, invoked by a valid session from an authorised workstation. There is no odd file, no
unknown process, no anomalous traffic towards the controller: there is a legitimate
command issued by the legitimate client.

**The industrial system's log does not distinguish it either.** From the system's point
of view, an operator opened a breaker. That is their job.

**And no industrial knowledge was needed for the final step.** All the specialist
knowledge came earlier: understanding the topology, knowing which breakers to open and
in what order. The act itself was point and click.

It is the same thesis that runs through half this site — the attacker uses what is
already there — taken to its most extreme form: **here what was already there was the
operator's screen.**

## 2. They attacked the recovery

This is the part that sets this incident apart from almost any other, and the one most
worth copying as a way of thinking.

Opening breakers is reversible: you close them. An attacker who only did that would
cause an outage of minutes. What turned this into six hours was a parallel campaign,
prepared in advance, against **the ability to respond**.

The last row is worth reading carefully, because it is the least technical and the most
revealing. **Flooding the telephone switchboard does nothing to the electrical grid.**
It does something to the process by which the company discovers the extent of its own
problem. It is an attack on the map, not the territory.

From here comes the statement this case contributes to the site:

> **Your response plan is a target.** If it is written down, somebody can read it. If it
> depends on a medium, that medium can be degraded beforehand. A prepared adversary does
> not only attack the process: they attack what you would use to recover it.

And the corresponding question, to be asked in the cold: **what does our response depend
on, and what happens if that is not there?** Case 009 reaches the same conclusion from a
ransomware that changes passwords; here it is done deliberately and against four
different means at once.

## 3. The IT to OT crossing was no feat

Another important correction. The step from the corporate network to the industrial one
was not a sophisticated breach. MITRE describes it with three techniques that, together,
are almost anticlimactic:

| Technique | What the record says |
|---|---|
| `T1018` Remote System Discovery | The OT systems **were visible from the IT network** |
| `T1040` Network Sniffing | Credentials travelling between the local network and the industrial systems were captured |
| `T0822` External Remote Services | Valid accounts taken from the domain controller were used to enter through **the control VPN the operators use** |

There is no exploitation of a vulnerability at the boundary between the two networks.
There is **visibility**, **credentials** and **legitimate remote access**.

For an analyst, that turns a vague question — "are we separated from OT?" — into three
verifiable ones:

1. From any workstation on the corporate network, what OT can be *seen*?
2. Are there OT credentials travelling over the corporate network, or working on both?
3. Who can use the control VPN, from where, and with what second factor?

All three are answered with inventory and packet captures, not with a diagram. And the
diagram almost always says the networks are separated.

## 4. Where a SOC's window was

This is the part that serves whoever has never seen an industrial system, and it is why
the case is worth it for everybody.

**Everything detectable happened on the IT side**, using techniques the rest of the site
has already covered:

| Moment | Technique | Where it is detected |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | `T1566.001` Spearphishing Attachment | Email, as in case 001 |
| Execution | `T1204.002` and `T1059.005` Office macro | Endpoint, as in case 010 |
| Command and control | `T1071.001` over HTTP, port 443 | Outbound traffic, as in case 010 |
| Credentials | `T1056.001` keylogger | Endpoint |
| Persistence | `T1136.002` new privileged domain accounts | Directory, and it is an extremely high-value alert |
| Movement | `T1570` tool transfer between IT and OT | Internal network |

None of those six requires a specialist in industrial protocols. They are ordinary SOC
detections, on an ordinary corporate network.

**The practical conclusion is encouraging and worth stating:** cutting this campaign did
not require understanding substations. It required detecting a macro, a command channel,
and **privileged domain accounts created outside an employee onboarding** — which is,
incidentally, the same alert that would have helped in case 002 and in case 011.

The window was months. That is the number that matters, not the six hours.

## 5. The restoration was manual

MITRE records it under `T0827`: operators were locked out of their equipment, and **much
of the power was restored manually.** Staff from the distributors travelled to the
substations to close breakers by hand.

That this was possible is no minor detail: it is why the outage lasted six hours and not
six days.

And it carries a warning about modernisation, worth stating without nostalgia. These
substations could be operated by hand because **there was something to operate with and
somebody who knew how**. An installation designed to be inoperable without software, or
where nobody remembers how, does not have that way out. It is the same question as case
009 — what part of the operation works without the systems that assist it — with an
answer that here saved the situation.

### The ICS matrix, which is where the damage happens

| MITRE ICS | Technique | In this case |
|---|---|---|
| **T0822** | External Remote Services | Domain controller accounts used on the control VPN |
| **T0859** | Valid Accounts | Interaction with client applications and HMI workstations |
| **T0846** | Remote System Discovery | Discovery of operational assets once inside the OT network |
| **T0823** | Graphical User Interface | **The SCADA interfaces used to open the breakers** |
| **T0886** | Remote Services | **Help desk software to move the mouse on the control machines** |
| **T0831** | Manipulation of Control | Live breakers opened by remote commands to the HMI |
| **T1692.001** | Unauthorized Message: Command Message | Unauthorised commands to substation breakers |
| **T1693.001** | Modify Firmware: System Firmware | Custom firmware on the serial-to-Ethernet converters |
| **T1695.001** | Block Communications: Serial COM | Communication with serial devices cut off or made harder |
| **T1691.001** and **.002** | Block OT Message: Command and Reporting | Neither commands downstream nor reports upstream |
| **T0813** | Denial of Control | Equipment needed for remote recovery rendered unusable |
| **T0814** | Denial of Service | Telephone flooding of the call centre |
| **T0816** | Device Restart/Shutdown | Power supply systems scheduled to shut down servers |
| **T0826** | Loss of Availability | Around six hours without supply |
| **T0827** | Loss of Control | Restoration largely manual |
| **T0828** | Loss of Productivity and Revenue | Inability to deliver power |

It is worth looking at the shape of the two tables together, because it says something
neither says alone. **The enterprise matrix covers the whole route and no damage. The ICS
matrix covers all the damage and no route.** A mapping done with only the first describes
one more intrusion; with only the second, a disaster with no cause. The incident needs
both, which is why industrial organisations need analysts who can read both.

It is the second case on the site to use the ICS matrix — the other is 09 — and the first
where it is the main map.

## Lessons learned

**There was no industrial malware at the moment of the damage.** The breakers were opened
from the SCADA's own interfaces, and partly by moving the operator's mouse with help desk
software. There is no signature for that, and the industrial system's log only sees an
operator doing their job.

**The response plan is a target.** Firmware overwritten, workstations wiped, servers shut
down from the power supply systems, and the call centre flooded. Four means of recovery
deliberately degraded in advance.

**Flooding the phones does not touch the grid: it touches the map.** It stops the company
knowing where the power is out. A reminder that the ability to find out is also an asset.

**The IT to OT crossing was visibility, credentials and a legitimate VPN.** There was no
exploitation at the boundary. That turns "are we separated?" into three verifiable
questions about what can be seen, which credentials travel, and who can use remote access.

**The detection window was entirely on the IT side, and it lasted months.** Macro, command
channel over 443, keylogger, privileged domain accounts created outside onboarding. None
requires a specialist in industrial protocols.

**Manual operation was the recovery.** It worked because there was something to work with
and somebody who knew how. An installation that cannot be operated without software has
no such way out.

**An industrial incident needs both matrices.** The enterprise one has the whole route and
no damage; the ICS one has all the damage and no route.

---

## Recommendations

- Inventory which OT assets are visible from the corporate network, measured with packet
  captures rather than with the network diagram
- Separate credentials between IT and OT, with no accounts that work on both, and no OT
  credentials travelling over the corporate network
- Remote access to the control network with a resistant second factor, restricted origins
  and session recording
- High-priority alert for the creation of privileged domain accounts outside employee
  onboarding
- Logging of the commands issued from operator workstations, with time and session, so a
  legitimate command can later be told apart from an imposed one
- Detection of remote access software on control network machines, where it has no reason
  to be
- Means of recovery that do not depend on the affected system: spare firmware, replacement
  equipment, and a customer communication channel other than the telephone
- Manual operating procedures written down, accessible offline, and staff trained to
  execute them
- Response exercises that include the deliberate degradation of the response means
  themselves
