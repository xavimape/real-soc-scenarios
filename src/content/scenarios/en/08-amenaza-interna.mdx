## Executive summary

Every previous case on this site begins the same way: somebody who should not be
inside gets in. This one does not.

Here there is no exploitation, no stolen credential, no tool that is not part of the
job. A person with legitimate access read records they have permission to read,
exported them with a feature the application offers, and copied them to a destination
policy does not block. **No individual action is a violation.** The combination is.

That changes the nature of the work. There is nothing bad to recognise: what has to
be measured is a difference from normal, and normal has to have been defined
beforehand.

And it changes something else, which is half the case. This is the only scenario where
the output of the analysis **is not a technical action but a file that goes to Human
Resources and Legal**, about an identifiable person, with consequences for their job
and potentially their liberty. An analyst who gets it wrong here does not produce a
false positive: they produce harm.

Which is why one rule organises the case: **the SOC investigates an activity, it does
not investigate a person.**

**Key metrics:**

- Observed actions that violate a policy on their own: none
- Deviation from the individual baseline: around 140 times
- Deviation from the peer group in the same role: 4.6 times
- Time from the alert to handover to HR and Legal: 6 hours
- Data the SOC looked up about the person's life: none
- Primary techniques: T1213, T1074.001, T1567.002

<Warn />**Synthetic scenario.** The organisation, the accounts and the logs were built
for the exercise. IP addresses come from the ranges RFC 5737 reserves for
documentation and the domains use the `.example` TLD from RFC 2606.

<Warn />**On the legal framing.** What follows describes working criteria, not legal
advice. What may be monitored, what may be retained and what has to be notified varies
a great deal by country, collective agreement and contract. The one recommendation that
holds everywhere is that those questions get answered **before** there is a case, and
they are answered by Legal, not by the SOC.

## Context: why this case is different

In an intrusion, the attacker has to obtain something they do not have: a credential,
a session, a permission. Each of those steps leaves an anomalous trace, and detections
are built on those traces.

An insider does not need to obtain anything. They already have it, and they use it the
way they do every day. The practical consequence:

**The left half of the detection work disappears.** No initial access, no escalation,
no lateral movement, no persistence. The case starts directly at collection and
exfiltration.

And a second, more uncomfortable consequence: since there is nothing forbidden to point
at, the only evidence available is **statistical**. Volume against a baseline. Timing
against a history. Destination against a habit. None of the three, alone, proves
anything. It is a kind of evidence that has to be presented without overstating it.

The last row is worth pausing on. **Every action, looked at alone, is part of the
job.** Reading the client register is the role. Exporting it is a capability the
profile includes. Uploading a file to cloud storage is something policy permits.

A triage that looks for the forbidden action will not find one, and will close the
ticket.

## 2. The individual baseline, and why it does not work here

The first instinct is right: compare against what this account normally does.

```
Account history, 12 months:   ~40 records per week  →  ~17 in three days
Observed, 16 to 18 April:     2,412 records in three days
Deviation:                    around 140 times
```

Note the intermediate step, which is easy to skip and changes the result by more than
double: the baseline is expressed per week and the observation per three days.
Comparing 2,412 against 40 gives sixty times and is a botched calculation. **Every
number with its denominator, including when the number favours the hypothesis.**

A hundred and forty times the baseline is an impressive figure, and it is **useless**.

On 1 April this person changed role. They moved from handling individual accounts to
leading a portfolio, and that role requires precisely what the baseline marks as
impossible: bulk access to the register.

There is a lesson here that gets paid for dearly in any behavioural detection
programme:

**A baseline built on a person expires when their role changes, and nothing in the
system says so.** The history is still there, still being computed, still producing
enormous deviations, and all of them are false. Worse still: they produce convincing
deviations, the kind that look decisive in a report.

From that comes a concrete requirement, and it is the most useful part of the case for
building detections: **the baseline has to be tied to the role, not to the personnel
record, and the detection system has to learn about role changes.** If HR moves
somebody and the behavioural engine does not know, that engine will generate false
positives about every person who changes post — which tend to be the people growing
inside the organisation.

---

## 3. The peer group

With the comparison against itself discarded, what remains is the one that applies:
against other people doing the same thing today.

```
Four other accounts in the portfolio lead role,
same three-day period:

  account A    512 records
  account B    480 records
  account C    697 records
  account D    534 records
  ──────────────────────────
  median       523 records

  m.ibarra   2,412 records   →  4.6 times the group median
```

Four and a half times the peer median. It is a real anomaly, and it is worth saying
precisely how strong it is: **it is far weaker than a hundred and forty times, and it
is the only one of the two that means anything.**

It is worth resisting the temptation to use the big number because it is more
persuasive. In a file that goes to Legal, a badly computed deviation that the other
side takes apart is worse than presenting nothing: it burns the credibility of the rest
of the evidence.

And even so, four times the median proves nothing on its own. Somebody can read four
times more than their peers because they were assigned a migration, because they are
covering for a colleague, or because they work differently. **The anomaly opens the
question; it does not answer it.**

---

## 4. The other two signals

What turns a volume anomaly into a case are the two that accompany it, and neither is
stronger than the first: what matters is that they point the same way.

**The timing.** Eighteen months of history for this account show activity between 08:30
and 18:00, with isolated exceptions. These three nights' readings happened between
22:40 and 01:15, three nights running. A role change does not explain a change of
working hours.

**The destination.** The exported files went to a personal cloud storage account, not
to an organisation resource. Policy does not block it, and that is a control failure
the case leaves on record. But the relevant point for the analysis is another: **no
task in the portfolio lead role requires taking the register outside the company's
systems.**

The three signals together — volume against peers, timing against history, destination
outside the perimeter — form a coherent picture. None is conclusive. All three pointing
the same way is the most technical evidence can contribute, and it is enough to
escalate.

It is not enough to conclude.

---

## 5. The limit: what the SOC does not do

Here is the part of the case that appears in no indicator and that determines whether
the analyst did their job.

With three signals pointing the same way, the temptation is to close the circle: read
the person's email, go through their messages, look at what sites they visited, check
whether they resigned. All of that is technically possible and in many environments the
SOC has the permissions to do it.

**It is not appropriate, and not mainly for abstract ethical reasons: for three
operational ones.**

**One, it destroys the evidence already held.** An investigation of a person conducted
without prior legal framing can render the entire file unusable, and turn the analyst
and the company into the defendant. Order matters: Legal authorises the scope first,
collection happens after.

**Two, it confuses the object.** What is being investigated is an activity on company
data. The moment the object becomes the person, the analysis starts looking for
confirmation instead of explanation, and finds it: any working life examined with
suspicion offers material.

**Three, the SOC does not have the context and should not have it.** The obvious
question — is this person about to leave? — has an authoritative answer, and Human
Resources holds it. The SOC does not read personnel files. It delivers the technical
fact and **HR contributes the context that belongs to it**, which in this scenario
turned out to be a resignation submitted four days before the first night.

That fact changes the reading entirely, and it is right that it arrived by that route
and not because an analyst went digging for it.

The formulation worth taking away: **the SOC produces facts; the conclusion about
intent is produced by somebody else, with more context and with the mandate to do it.**

## 6. How the report is written

In this kind of case the language of the ticket is part of the technical work, because
the ticket will be read by someone in Legal and possibly by a third party.

| Not like this | Like this |
|---|---|
| "Ibarra stole the client portfolio" | "The m.ibarra account queried 2,412 records between 16 and 18 April" |
| "She took the data before resigning" | "The queries happened between 22:40 and 01:15, outside the account's usual hours according to 18 months of history" |
| "Clearly she was planning to join a competitor" | "The exported files were uploaded to a personal cloud storage account" |
| "Plainly malicious activity" | "The activity deviates 4.6 times from the median of the peer group in the same role" |

Three rules behind that table:

**The subject is the account, not the person.** Who operates an account is a reasonable
inference and not an observed fact. Writing "the account" is not a euphemism: it is
precision.

**Verbs of observation, not of intent.** "Queried", "exported", "was uploaded". Never
"stole", "planned", "concealed".

**Every number with its denominator.** "2,412 records" says nothing; "2,412 against a
median of 523 in the same period and the same role" says everything. And if the
deviation was computed against a baseline that no longer applies, it does not go in.

## 7. The innocent version of the same case

The exercise is worth running in reverse, because it protects against the worst
possible failure.

The same three signals — volume four times over peers, night-time hours, export to
personal storage — are exactly what is produced by a person who was asked to migrate
the register to a new tool, who works at night because during the day the system is
loaded, and who uses their personal storage because nobody gave them a corporate one
with enough space.

**The technical data are identical in both scenarios.** There is no query, no tool and
no correlation that separates them, because the difference is not in the activity but
in the authorisation, and the authorisation lives outside the SOC.

This is the same structure as case 06: the ambiguous alert is not resolved by looking
at it more closely, it is resolved by asking whoever holds the authoritative answer.
The difference is the cost of skipping that step. There, an incident that keeps
running. Here, an accusation against somebody who may have done nothing.

Which is why the handover to HR and Legal is neither bureaucracy nor passing the
problem along. **It is the only step in the procedure that can tell the two scenarios
apart.**

### Breakdown

| MITRE ID | Technique | In this case |
|---|---|---|
| **T1213** | Data from Information Repositories | The 2,412 queries against the client register |
| **T1074.001** | Data Staged: Local Data Staging | The three exported files gathered before uploading |
| **T1567.002** | Exfiltration Over Web Service: Exfiltration to Cloud Storage | The upload to personal storage |

And something is worth saying about the map this case leaves behind, because **the
absence is the data**.

Two tactics. The previous cases on this site use between three and five, and always
include something from the left half of the matrix: initial access, execution,
persistence, escalation, lateral movement. Here there is none, and not because the
investigation fell short: **they did not happen.** Whoever holds the credential does
not need to obtain it, and whoever is already where the data is does not need to move.

ATT&CK models the behaviour of an adversary who has to work their way in. An insider
threat case begins past that road, so the framework has little to say about the part
that matters — volume, timing, destination, authorisation — and that is not a defect of
the framework but a limit of its scope.

The practical reading: **an ATT&CK map that only touches collection and exfiltration
is, in itself, a signal that you are facing an actor who was already inside with
permission.** It works as a triage question.

## Lessons learned

**The SOC investigates an activity, not a person.** It is the rule that orders
everything else. It protects whoever is innocent, it protects the validity of the file
when they are not, and it protects the analyst.

**An individual baseline expires with a role change, and nobody says so.** The
deviation from history was enormous, convincing and false. The baseline has to be tied
to the current role, and the detection engine has to learn when that role changes.

**The peer group is the comparison that applies.** Four and a half times the median of
those doing the same job today is a smaller number and the only one that means
anything.

**Three weak signals pointing the same way is everything technical evidence can give.**
Volume, timing and destination. None concludes. Together they are enough to escalate,
not to accuse.

**The language of the report is technical work.** Subject is the account, verbs of
observation, every number with its denominator. A badly computed deviation that the
other side takes apart burns the credibility of all the evidence.

**The same data describe somebody guilty and somebody doing their job.** The difference
is in the authorisation, and the authorisation lives outside the SOC. The handover is
not bureaucracy: it is the only step that separates the two scenarios.

**An ATT&CK map that only touches collection and exfiltration is a signal in itself.**
The empty left half indicates an actor who was already inside with permission.

---

## Recommendations

- Baseline by current role, fed by post changes, rather than by individual history from
  the personnel record
- Automatic peer group comparison as part of the alert, so the analyst does not have to
  build it by hand
- A written handover procedure to HR and Legal, with the scope of what the SOC may
  collect before that authorisation explicitly bounded
- Review the personal cloud storage policy: in this case it allowed the data out with no
  friction, and it is the most concrete control failure in the incident
- A dedicated alert for bulk export from business applications, separate from the read
  volume alert
- Permission review on role change, including removal of the previous ones, which this
  case does not exercise but which the same mechanics make obvious
- Define with Legal, before a case exists, what may be monitored, how long it is
  retained and what has to be notified
- Specific team training on writing reports that go to other departments
