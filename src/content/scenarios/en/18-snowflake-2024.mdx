## Executive summary

Between April and June 2024, roughly **165 organisations** were notified that someone
had walked into their cloud data warehouse and taken what was inside.

No vulnerability was exploited. No malware ran on the victims' infrastructure. Nothing
was encrypted, there was no ransom note on a screen, no service went down. The attacker
connected with a username and a password, ran queries the product documents in its
manual, exported the results and then called the victims to ask for money.

**The credentials were not stolen in 2024.** Many came from infostealer infections on
machines that did not belong to those companies — personal computers, contractor
laptops — and the oldest one identified dated back to **November 2020.** It still
worked four years later.

This case is the exact reverse of 017. There the attacker came in through the one path
authorised not to raise suspicion. Here they came through the front door with the right
key, and **the SOC's problem is not detecting an intrusion but telling an intruder
apart from a data analyst doing their job.**

**Key metrics:**

- Organisations notified: around 165
- Accounts used that had prior credential exposure: 79.7 per cent
- Oldest infostealer infection identified: November 2020
- Age of unrotated credentials, in some cases: up to four years
- Vulnerabilities exploited in the platform: none
- Main techniques: T1555, T1078.004, T1213.002, T1530, T1657

<Warn />**Real case.** The technical facts, figures and commands come from Mandiant's
report on UNC5537 of 10 June 2024 and from the detection and hardening guidance
published by Snowflake. The joint investigation by both companies concluded there was
no compromise of the platform: every incident traced back to customer credentials.

## Context: where the credentials were stolen

An infostealer is a program that installs on a computer, empties everything it finds
saved — browser passwords, session cookies, tokens, configuration files — and ships it
to a server. That material is then sold, given away or leaked in lists that circulate
for years.

What makes this case different is **where those infections happened.**

```
NOT on the victim company's network
NOT on a machine managed by its IT department
NOT on a device with detection installed

YES on personal computers
YES on contractor machines also used for private activity
YES on machines where pirated software was downloaded, or games were played
```

Mandiant states it plainly: in several investigations, the infection occurred on
contractor systems that were also used for personal activity.

That detail matters to an analyst more than it appears. **The event that originated the
incident did not happen on any network the SOC can watch**, produced no alert anyone
could have seen, and happened years earlier. By the time the intrusion arrives, there
is nothing prior to review.

And there is a worse consequence. A contractor working for several organisations
carries credentials for all of them on the same computer. A single infection reaches
them all, often with administrative privileges.

## 1. The three conditions, and why all three are configuration

The report attributes the campaign's success to three factors. None of them is a
vulnerability.

**First: the accounts had no second factor.** Authenticating required only a valid
username and password, which is exactly what the attacker had.

**Second: the credentials were still alive.** Material stolen in 2020 worked in 2024.
In some cases the passwords had gone four years without rotation.

**Third: there were no network allow lists.** The platform lets you restrict which
addresses can reach an instance. Without that restriction, a connection from a
commercial VPN exit on another continent is as valid as one from the office.

<Fail />None of the three is a product defect: all three are decisions made by whoever
configured it.

<Pass />And for that same reason all three were reversible before the incident, without
buying anything.

It is worth pausing on the uncomfortable part. A notice like this usually gets read as
"such-and-such vendor got hacked". Here the vendor was not compromised, and the joint
investigation established that. **What failed was configuration on the customer side,
which is where the SOC does have reach.**

## 2. What a theft made of manual commands looks like

This is the part worth reading slowly, because it is what an analyst would have in
front of them.

The observed sequence was always similar:

```sql
SHOW TABLES                            -- list what is there
SELECT * FROM <db>.<schema>.<table>    -- look at what matters
LIST <stage>                           -- see where material can be left
CREATE TEMPORARY STAGE <name>          -- create a temporary area
COPY INTO @<stage> FROM (SELECT ...)   -- copy the table there, compressed
GET @<stage> file:///<local path>      -- download it
```

None of those six commands is suspicious on its own. All six are documented, all six
are used by legitimate people every day, and none trips an antivirus because there is
no file to analyse.

**The signal is not in the commands, it is in the shape.** What separates an intruder
from a data analyst:

- A `SHOW TABLES` followed by `SELECT` against tables that account never queried
- Temporary staging areas created by an account that never creates any
- Export volume out of proportion with that account's own baseline
- A connecting client different from the usual one
- All of it in a single session, at an hour when that person does not work

Each point on its own is weak. **Together they describe someone seeing that warehouse
for the first time**, and that is a description that fits nobody who works there.

The attacker also used a bespoke reconnaissance utility that queried users, roles,
addresses and sessions. Mandiant tracks it as FROSTBITE; the name its author gave it is
not reproduced here. And they also used a commercial, entirely legitimate database
client, the kind anyone downloads.

## 4. The step that is not there

In almost every previous case there is a moment of escalation: the attacker gets in
with little and obtains more. Here there is none, and the absence teaches something.

The stolen account already had access to the data. **There was no need to escalate
because the objective was within reach of the first access.** A data warehouse exists
precisely to concentrate information, and the accounts that query it have permission to
query it.

That changes two things in the response:

- **There is no intermediate detection window.** In a classic intrusion, between first
  access and objective there is lateral movement, escalation, persistence: stages where
  you can look. Here the first useful event and the impact are minutes apart.
- **Containment is revocation, and it has to be fast.** There are no machines to
  isolate and no processes to kill. You cut sessions, rotate credentials and close
  network access. All of it is reversible and cheap, and none of it requires confirming
  scope first.

---

## 5. Extortion without encryption

The ending breaks the mould too. After exporting, the attacker offered the data on
forums and contacted the victims for money.

Nothing encrypted. No service interrupted. **The leverage is not availability, it is
exposure.**

For a SOC that has a practical consequence: the classic indicators of a serious
incident — systems down, unreadable files, users unable to work — do not appear. The
organisation keeps operating normally while its data is already outside. The first
signal may arrive by email from the attacker, or from a third party who saw the records
for sale.

Case 006 on this site works on ransomware precursors, and there the question is whether
you get there before the encryption. Here **there is no "after" to prevent**: the damage
is done at download, and everything that follows is negotiation.

## Lessons learned

**A stolen credential does not expire on its own.** The only moment a leaked password
stops working is when someone changes it. Four years is ample time for material to
travel from a casual infection to an organised campaign.

**The machine where the credential is stolen may not be yours, and usually is not.**
This breaks the assumption that your own telemetry is enough. Against this vector, your
own telemetry sees absolutely nothing until the moment of access.

**Contractors concentrate several organisations' risk on a single device.** An
unmanaged machine, used for work and for personal activity, carrying credentials for
five clients: that is the most valuable asset in the chain and the least watched.

**The second factor is not just another control.** Of the three conditions that made
the campaign possible, it is the one that alone would have stopped it, and the cheapest
of the three.

**Severity is not measured in sophistication.** This case used no remarkable technique
and reached a hundred and sixty-five organisations. A thin ATT&CK mapping does not mean
a minor incident.

---

## Recommendations

**Mandatory second factor on all data access, with no exceptions for convenience.**
Documented exceptions are the list an attacker would want to have, as case 003 already
pointed out.

**Credential rotation with a deadline, and exposure checking.** Services exist that
report whether an organisation's credential has appeared in stolen material. Querying
them periodically is cheap and answers a question your own telemetry cannot.

**Network allow lists on data warehouses.** Not a perfect defence, but it turns a stolen
credential into something that also requires being in the right place.

**A baseline per account, not per organisation.** What gives this attacker away is the
disproportion against what that account normally does. Without an individual baseline,
export volume says nothing.

**Alert on the combination, not on the commands.** Temporary staging area created,
`COPY INTO` and `GET` in the same session, by an account that never does it. Each piece
is routine; the sequence is not.

**Treat contractors as your own surface.** Access from managed devices, or a second
factor and network restriction that compensate for their not being managed.
