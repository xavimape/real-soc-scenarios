## Executive summary

On 31 July 2026, the company operating the pipeline that carries most of the oil out of
Vaca Muerta told the securities regulator that it had identified **an information
security incident** affecting certain administrative systems.

The crude never stopped for a minute. The company applied its protocol, restored all of
its administrative systems and stated that it **was still assessing the definitive
scope**. What matters to an analyst is the rest of the sentence: the incident reached
administration and **did not reach the systems that move the oil.**

But this case is here for another reason, and it is worth stating up front.

**There is no forensic report. The entry vector is unknown. The company did not say what
kind of threat it was — in fact, it never used the word "attack". One group claimed the
event eight days before the company disclosed it, a different group turned up claiming
it a week later, and neither thing is confirmed.**

That is the normal situation for an analyst in the region: an incident at critical
infrastructure with nothing but a three-paragraph regulatory filing, press reports that
contradict each other, and criminals' posts that nobody verified. **The case is how you
work with that without inventing what is missing.**

**Key metrics:**

- Claim recorded on a leak site: 23 July 2026, 15:13 UTC
- Official filing with the regulator: 31 July 2026, 15:50, filing 3551175
- Distance between the two: eight days
- Declared impact on crude transport: none
- Kilometres of pipeline operated: more than 1,700
- Publicly known entry vector: none
- Ransom payment reported: none
- Share the group offers its affiliates: 90 per cent of the ransom
- Edge devices the group is estimated to have already compromised, per a research
  firm: around 14,700

<Warn />**Real case, with scarce information and sources that do not agree.** The facts
come from the material-event filing with the National Securities Commission, from the
technical analyses of the group published by Trend Micro, Microsoft Threat Intelligence
and Halcyon, from the public record of the claim and from Argentine press coverage. Contradictions between
sources are noted where they appear, without picking one. What is unknown is stated as
such.

## Context: why this company matters

The company transports liquid hydrocarbons from the Neuquén basin to the export
terminals on the Atlantic. Its shareholders are the state-controlled oil company, with
37 per cent, and the main operators in the country and the world. Its customers are,
essentially, every producer in the play.

```
Vaca Muerta fields
        │
        ▼
more than 1,700 km of pipeline ──► domestic refineries
        │
        └───────────────────────► export terminals
```

That concentration is the point of the system and also its fragility: **funnelling a
whole basin's output through a single operator is efficient, and it turns that operator
into a national matter.**

One clarification the case needs: the share of crude it carries is published two ways.
The official filing and most of the press say **75 per cent** of Vaca Muerta's output;
other industry sources estimate close to **90 per cent**. They are not the same, and
there is no public way to settle it. When a case opens with a discrepancy in its first
figure, treat it as a preview of what follows.

## 1. The only thing the company asserted

The regulatory filing is the sole first-hand source, and it is worth reading for what it
says and for what it does not.

Starting with how it is catalogued, which already says something. In the regulator's own
form, the filing was recorded like this:

```
Date            31/07/2026, 15:50
Type            Events or situations affecting the entity's activity
Detail          Other
Summary         Cybersecurity incident, Oleoductos del Valle
Filing          3551175
```

**The disclosure regime has no category for this.** The form's menu offers "mass
redundancies", "casualties" and "other", and a cyberattack on critical infrastructure
comes in under the last one. That is not a bureaucratic detail: **what a regime does not
classify, it does not measure**, and that is why there is no public statistic on
cybersecurity incidents at Argentina's listed companies.

The full text fits in one paragraph, and it is worth reading as written:

> The Company has identified an information security incident, which affected certain
> administrative systems, with no impact or service interruption to the transport
> operation. The Company applied its cybersecurity protocol and managed to restore all
> administrative systems, allowing administrative activity to resume normally. The
> Company continues to assess the definitive scope of the incident and is formalising
> the corresponding filings with the competent authorities.

It is signed by the officer holding the Market Relations Officer role and addressed to
the regulator and to the two markets where the company lists debt.

### What it says, in the words it chose

**"Information security incident", not "cyberattack".** The difference is not stylistic.
An attack implies a third party acting with intent; an incident can be many things. The
company **never asserted it had been attacked.**

**"Certain administrative systems", not "the administrative systems".** It narrowed the
scope with an adjective, without saying which ones.

**"Continues to assess the definitive scope of the incident".** As of 31 July, the
company itself was stating that it **did not yet fully know what had happened.**

### What it does not say, and that needs saying

- What kind of threat it was.
- How they got in.
- Whether data was exfiltrated.
- How long they were inside.
- Whether a ransom was demanded.
- The phrase **"100% controlled"**, which the press attributes to this document.

### The detail that turns this case into an exercise

That last line deserves a pause, because it is the heart of the matter.

**The phrase "100% controlled" is not in the filing.** It appears in quotation marks
across much of the coverage, presented as what the company told the regulator. It may
come from later statements to the press; what is certain is that it is not in the
document.

And there is a tension between that phrase and what the document does say. "100%
controlled" suggests a closed incident. The text says the Company **continues to assess
the definitive scope**. Those are two different assertions about the same event, and the
more reassuring one has no documentary backing.

<Fail />Repeating "100% controlled" as the official position means reproducing a quote
without checking it against its source.

<Pass />Reading the document takes three minutes and it is available to anyone on the
regulator's site.

**This is not a criticism of the press, it is the demonstration of why the rule exists.**
A phrase gets repeated, it settles in, and by the third article nobody remembers that
nobody verified it. An analyst who builds a report out of press clippings inherits that
error and signs it as their own.

## 2. The separation that worked

This is the technical finding of the case, and it is good news worth understanding
properly so as not to draw the wrong conclusion.

At a company that moves hydrocarbons, two worlds coexist:

```
INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY              OPERATIONAL TECHNOLOGY
email, billing, records             pumps, valves, telemetry
users coming and going              processes that do not stop
patched on a Tuesday                patched during a planned outage
```

The attack stayed on the left. **That does not happen by accident: it happens when
someone designed the separation and maintained it.**

Now, the wrong conclusion would be "so nothing happened". Three reasons not to draw it:

**First: the administrative side matters too.** That is where contracts, dispatched
volumes, customer information and personnel data live. Exfiltrating that material is a
problem even if no valve moves.

**Second: separation is tested, not declared.** That the attacker did not reach the
other side in this incident says nothing about how hard it would have been. That
question is answered by looking at the architecture, not at the outcome.

**Third, and the one most worth looking at: continuing to operate is a decision.** In
2021 the operator of the largest pipeline in the United States suffered an attack that
was **also on the administrative side**, and stopped pumping anyway as a precaution,
because it could not bill and because it was not sure how separated its network was.
That shutdown caused fuel shortages on the east coast.

Three cases, three different endings to the same question:

| Case | Where the attacker got to | What happened to operations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ukraine 2015, case 013 | The operator's own console | They opened the breakers by hand |
| United States 2021 | Administration only | The company stopped it itself |
| This case | Administration only | It did not stop |

**The difference between the last two rows is not technical, it is confidence.** You
stop operations when you are not sure about the separation. You keep going when you
are. And that certainty is built beforehand, with architecture and rehearsals, not
during the incident.

## 3. What a claim is worth

Here is the part no other case on this site teaches.

A ransomware group listed this company on its leak site on 23 July. The company
disclosed the attack on the 31st. It is tempting to join the two and close the matter.
**Do not, and it is worth understanding why.**

**A post on a leak site is a criminal's assertion, not evidence.** Groups:

- Claim attacks they did not carry out, to inflate their standing with affiliates
- Exaggerate the scope of the ones they did
- Repost old data from other leaks as if it were new
- Sometimes list a victim who negotiated and paid, to pressure others

Two different groups claiming the same company within two weeks admits several
readings, and none can be ruled out with the information available:

1. There were two separate attacks by two separate groups. It happens, especially when
   initial access is bought and resold.
2. One group took credit for another's work.
3. One of the two, or both, are claiming something they did not do.
4. The same affiliate worked for two programs at different times.

**The honest thing is to write that down and not choose.** An analyst who reports "the
company was attacked by group X" because they read it on a tracker is reporting an
attacker's assertion as if it were their own finding.

The correct formulation has this shape: *a group identifying itself as X listed the
organisation on its leak site on such a date; the organisation did not confirm the
attribution; there is no independent public evidence supporting it.* It is longer, and
it is the only one that survives a follow-up question.

## 4. What can be asserted about the named attacker

About the group that claimed it, on the other hand, **there is abundant, good-quality
research**, published by several firms between September 2025 and 2026. The two things
must be kept apart: **what follows describes the group and its methods, not this
particular attack.** None of these techniques is confirmed at this company.

### Where it came from

It is not a new group, it is a splinter. Its members previously operated as affiliates
of another ransomware program, and the break came over money: in July 2025 one of them
publicly complained on an underground forum that he was owed some 48,000 dollars in
commissions. Four days before that complaint, the first sample of their own encryptor
had already appeared, with the address of their leak site embedded inside. **The split
was prepared before the fight went public.**

Their business model is what explains the scale: they offer affiliates **90 per cent**
of the ransom, where the market norm is between 70 and 80. With that incentive they went
from not existing to accumulating hundreds of victims across dozens of countries in
under a year, and most recent attacks are carried out by affiliates rather than the core
team.

One detail of that profile matters for this case: **the geographic distribution of their
victims departs from the ecosystem average.** Only a small fraction is in the United
States, where ransomware as a whole concentrates about half its victims. One research
firm reads that deviation as a sign that their affiliate base draws from Southeast Asia
and Latin America. Right or not, it fits two oil-sector victims in the region in the
same month.

### How they get in

Here is the finding that helps a defender most, and the one that connects this case to
case 020.

**The main documented vector is an edge device**: an authentication bypass flaw in the
operating system of a widely deployed firewall vendor, which lets an attacker with no
credentials skip the login, create administrator accounts and stay. In a Trend Micro
investigation of one of the group's incidents, the entry point was exactly that: an
appliance from that vendor **reachable from the internet**, with the internal scanning
tools later run from its administrative account profile.

The rest of the chain, in that same incident, is textbook:

```
exposed edge ──► reconnaissance with legitimate scanning tools
                 mass domain account enumeration with a .bat script
                 abuse of a signed, vulnerable driver to kill the antivirus
                 bespoke tool built against the environment's security product
                 commercial remote access installed as a persistent channel
                 manipulation of domain group policies
                 exfiltration via an encrypted transfer utility
                 encryptor deployed from the domain's logon share
```

Two things on that list are worth pausing on.

**First: they changed tools mid-attack.** They started with a generic antivirus-killing
utility, looked at which security product was in the environment, and then deployed a
variant purpose-built against that product. This is not a template attack: someone is
watching and adapting.

**Second: the final deployment went through the domain logon share**, which reaches
every machine joined to it. Combined with group policy manipulation, that turns a domain
administrator compromise into simultaneous encryption of the whole organisation.

### The date of the impact is not the date of entry

And this is the most uncomfortable fact of all. One research firm estimates the group
maintains an **inventory of around 14,700 already-compromised edge devices**, plus about
a thousand VPN credentials obtained by brute force, ready to use whenever it suits them.

If that is so, the consequence for any investigation is significant: **the day the
encryption appears may be very far from the day they got in.** An analysis that starts
its timeline at the date of impact will look for initial access in the wrong window.

### What the encryptor does

On the tool itself there is a detailed analysis by Microsoft Threat Intelligence, which
tracks the operators as Storm-2697. Written in Go and obfuscated, with per-file
encryption using ephemeral Curve25519 keys and the XChaCha20 stream cipher. Before
encrypting it disables the antivirus's real-time protection, adds itself to the
exclusions, deletes shadow copies, event logs and command history. It persists through
two scheduled tasks and two registry keys.

And then there is the propagation. With one command-line argument, the encryptor **stops
being a program and becomes a worm**: it sets up a hidden share to serve itself, carries
an embedded legitimate remote execution tool, enumerates the network and against each
discovered machine attempts **twenty-one distinct remote execution operations** — copy
over the administrative share, remote execution, process creation via management
instrumentation, three user scheduled tasks, three system ones, three services,
PowerShell remoting and process creation by another route.

**It attempts all of them, regardless of whether the earlier ones failed.**

That is the detail to take away. A defence that blocks ninety per cent of lateral
movement methods does not reduce risk by ninety per cent: **one working method on one
machine is enough for the propagation to continue.**

### An aside on why so much is known

In May 2026 the group itself suffered a leak of its internal database, exposing operator
accounts and working conversations. Much of the detail about how they organise, which
vulnerabilities they were evaluating and how they negotiate comes from there.

It works as a reminder of something easily forgotten: **threat intelligence has
provenance too, and this group's improved a great deal the day what they do to others
happened to them.**

## Analysis frameworks

**This case carries none of the three, and the reason is the case itself.**

The **kill chain** needs to know how they got in and how far they went. Neither is
known; seven empty boxes are not an analysis.

The **pyramid of pain** ranks indicators by what it costs the attacker to change them.
There is not a single public indicator from this incident.

The **Diamond Model** needs data at all four vertices. There is a victim, there is
capability described by a solid third party, there is a **presumed and unconfirmed**
adversary, and there is absolutely nothing about the infrastructure used in this attack.
Two vertices out of four, and one of those in doubt.

This site's invariant is that a framework goes in when there is real data to fill it.
There is none here, and **saying so is more honest than drawing a diamond with two
invented vertices.** A case without frameworks teaches something too: it teaches how to
tell what is known from what is assumed.

## Lessons learned

**The separation between administration and operations is what decides the size of the
incident.** Here the difference between a nuisance and a national problem was that
boundary, and it has to be tested beforehand, not verified during.

**Continuing to operate during an incident is a decision made with information.** If you
do not know how separated the networks are, the prudent move is to stop, and stopping
has a cost too. That information is built in peacetime.

**The edge is the door, and case 020 already said so.** This group's documented route
comes in through an exposed, unpatched firewall. Two cases in the catalogue, two
different vendors and three years apart, end up pointing at the same box: the one bought
to protect.

**The date of the impact is not the date of entry.** A group holding thousands of bought
or stolen accesses in reserve can use them months later. A timeline that starts when the
encryption appears looks for initial access in the wrong window.

**A quote is checked against its source, not against another article.** The most repeated
phrase in this case is not in the document it is attributed to, and it contradicts what
that document does say. Three minutes of reading separate a verified quote from an
inherited one.

**The words a company chooses are evidence.** "Incident" is not "attack", "certain
systems" is not "the systems", and "continues to assess" is not "controlled". Whoever
drafts a regulatory filing weighs every term, and the analyst reading it should weigh
them the same way.

**A claim is not an attribution.** It is a criminal's assertion about their own work,
published where it suits them to be read. It works as a lead and as a warning; it does
not work as a conclusion.

**When there is no report, honest analysis has declared gaps.** Filling in an entry
vector with what usually happens is writing fiction in the format of a report.

**That a piece of data is accessible does not make it publishable.** Trackers publish
exposure data on identifiable victims; using it to prepare yourself is legitimate,
spreading it is not.

**In Argentina there is no obligation to say more.** A 2019 resolution defines sectors
of critical information infrastructure, but it does not identify specific operators or
set enforceable requirements, and compelling private companies would require a law that
does not exist. **The consequence is exactly this case: with no rule, what anyone learns
from an incident at critical infrastructure depends on the victim's goodwill.**

---

## Recommendations

**Treat edge devices as the critical asset they are.** Inventory with version and
owner, patching with a deadline, review of the administrator accounts that exist on
them, and a phishing-resistant second factor on the VPN. If an appliance sat exposed and
unpatched during a known window, audit it before calling it healthy.

**Verify the separation with evidence**, not with the network diagram. What path exists
today between administration and operations, who uses it, and what happens if that
account is compromised.

**Define the threshold for stopping operations in advance**, and who decides. Written
down, with a name and a deputy.

**Harden against redundant lateral movement.** Against a tool that tries twenty-one
routes per machine, blocking most is not enough: close the remote execution paths that
are not used and alert on remote service and task creation.

**Check your own credential exposure** with monitoring services. It is cheap and answers
a question internal telemetry cannot.

**Write your own reports with the line between fact and assumption marked.** "Group X
claimed" and "group X attacked" are different assertions, and whoever reads the report
will make decisions on that difference.

**Take part in the sector's information-sharing mechanisms**, which in this case would
have flagged that the same group had hit a regional oil company days earlier.
