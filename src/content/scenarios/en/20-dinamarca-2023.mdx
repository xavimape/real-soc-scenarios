## Executive summary

In May 2023, twenty-two companies operating parts of the Danish energy infrastructure
were compromised in a coordinated attack. Several had to switch to island operation,
disconnecting from the internet in order to keep running.

The entry point was the firewall. **The very device separating the industrial control
systems from the rest of the world was the one carrying the vulnerability**, and a
single packet to port 500 over UDP was enough to execute commands with maximum
privileges without knowing any credentials.

But what makes this case unique is not the vulnerability. It is **where it is told
from.** The other nineteen cases on this site look at one organisation. This one is
written by a sector CERT that watched three hundred members with a network of two
hundred and seventy sensors, and that saw the pattern because it could compare across
companies what, at each one individually, might have gone unnoticed.

**The question in this case is what can be seen from above that cannot be seen from
inside**, and what a team does when it has sixteen simultaneous incidents and no staff
to handle them.

**Key metrics:**

- Companies compromised in total: 22
- First-wave targets: 16, of which 11 compromised
- Members of the sector organisation: around 300
- Sensors deployed: 270
- Score of the first vulnerability: 9.8 out of 10
- Days between the vendor advisory and the attack: 16
- Main techniques: T1190, T0819, T1584.005, T1498

<Warn />**Real case, with the separation of fact and analysis made by the source
itself.** The SektorCERT report explicitly divides what was observed from what was
interpreted, and warns that its analysis is subjective and may be wrong. That separation
is respected here: where something is assessment rather than observation, it is said.

## Context: the firewall as the only control

At many small plants, the separation between industrial control systems and the internet
is made by a single device.

```
internet  ──►  firewall  ──►  industrial control systems
                  ▲
                  └── the only control, and the one with the vulnerability
```

That design is not carelessness: it is what a small company operating a stretch of
electrical or heating network can afford. And it works, as long as the device is
healthy.

When the vulnerability is in the control itself, **defence in depth becomes zero.**
There is no second layer to compensate, because the second layer was that one. An
organisation monitoring alerts on its internal network sees nothing, because the
attacker is not on the internal network yet: they are at the door.

An organisation dedicated to monitoring the internet put it bluntly when the advisory
came out: if you have a vulnerable device exposed, assume compromise.

## 1. Why they were not updated

The report lists the reasons given by the affected companies themselves, and they are
the most usable material in the case because **none of them is negligence in the sense
we usually mean:**

- **"The device is new, it comes updated."** A reasonable and false assumption.
- **"The vendor handles updates."** Nobody had verified it in writing.
- **"The update costs money."** The software was free, but the vendor charged to install
  it, and someone decided to postpone.
- **"We did not know we had that device."** Installed by a supplier without telling
  anyone, or simply no inventory at all.

<Fail />All four are management problems, not security problems, and none is solved by
buying a detection tool.

<Pass />All four are answered by one question, asked in time: **who updates this device
and how do we verify it?**

The fourth is the most serious and the most common in practice. The camera supplier who
left a firewall installed without saying so is not an anecdote: it is the usual way
uninventoried equipment appears on a network. Every third-party installation — cameras,
access control, climate, metering — can bring in a network device nobody recorded.

**An inventory built by asking IT is incomplete by design.** It has to be built by
looking at the network.

## 2. What the attack needed, and what it did not

The first vulnerability was exploited **with a single packet** sent to port 500 over
UDP. That packet was processed by the key exchange protocol's decoder, and that is where
the flaw was. The result was command execution with maximum privileges, with no username
and no password.

It is worth pausing on what that means for detection:

- **There is no failed authentication attempt.** The access log shows nothing.
- **There is no prior scanning traffic.** No sweep preceded the attack.
- **One packet is not a pattern.** Detections based on volume or repetition have nothing
  to hold on to.

On the eleven compromised devices, the first thing the attackers did was ask the
firewall for its configuration and its usernames. The CERT's assessment is that this was
reconnaissance: seeing how each one was set up before deciding how to proceed.

**That does leave a signal.** An edge device suddenly sending its configuration outbound
is a rare event, observable in traffic, and it does not depend on knowing the
vulnerability.

## 3. What can only be seen from above

Here is what sets this case apart from every other one on the site.

The attackers picked sixteen targets and **hit all sixteen.** All of them did have the
vulnerable device. The report notes something unsettling: at the time, information about
who had vulnerable devices was not available on the public search engines for exposed
equipment, and the CERT could not identify any prior scanning in its data that would
have provided it. To this day there is no clear explanation.

The simultaneity is not accidental either. **Attacking everyone at once neutralises
information sharing**, which is a sector's main defence: by the time the first victim
notices, there is nobody left to warn.

Against that tactic, the only thing that works is seeing everyone at the same time. And
that is exactly what the sensor network did: identify the pattern by crossing data
between companies, when each individual attack **might have gone unnoticed inside its
own organisation.**

## 4. The prize was the device, not the plant

There is a twist that makes the case more useful than it looks.

After compromising the firewalls, much of the second wave **did not head for the control
systems.** It added the devices to networks of compromised machines and used them
against third parties: denial of service against targets in the United States and Hong
Kong, SSH brute force against a company in Canada.

The CERT's reading is that this was, all things considered, good news: it suggests those
attackers wanted the device and not the critical infrastructure behind it, which they
may have reached without realising.

For an analyst there are two practical conclusions:

**First: a compromised edge device has value in itself.** The attacker does not need to
understand or care about what is behind it. That vastly widens the set of people who can
attack a plant: no industrial systems specialists required.

**Second: the most visible signal was outbound traffic.** In one case the volume
saturated the firewall itself and left the company without a network. Watching what
leaves an edge device — not only what enters it — is cheap and is what set off several
alarms.

---

## 5. Cutting the connection as a defensive decision

Several companies switched to island operation: they disconnected from the internet and
kept operating.

It is the most drastic measure available and in this case it was the right one. It is
worth looking at closely because **it contradicts the usual reflex of maintaining
service at all costs.**

In an industrial environment the question is not "can we stay connected?" but "can we
keep operating without a connection?". If the answer is yes, disconnecting is cheap. If
it is no, that in itself is a serious finding, and one better discovered in a workshop
than during an incident.

In one case the firewall had to be replaced entirely: it could not be trusted back into
service. **When the compromised device is the one enforcing the security control,
cleaning it is not a reasonable option.**

## Lessons learned

**When the control is the vulnerable thing, there is no second layer.** An edge firewall
protecting an industrial environment concentrates all the risk, and updating it stops
being maintenance and becomes a critical control.

**The inventory that is missing is the one a third party installed.** Cameras, access
control, climate: any job can leave behind a network device nobody recorded. An
inventory built by asking will not find those devices; one built by looking at the
network will.

**"The vendor handles it" is a verifiable claim, and it needs verifying.** Preferably in
writing and with a date.

**Attacking everyone at once neutralises information sharing.** The only defence against
that tactic is simultaneous observation, which no organisation can have over itself.

**An edge device is worth having in itself.** The attacker may have no interest in the
plant at all, and that vastly widens who might end up compromising it.

**What cut the chain was a management decision.** Working outside office hours with no
staffing for it appears in no security architecture, and it was decisive.

---

## Recommendations

**An edge device inventory built from the network**, not from memory and not from
purchase orders. With make, model, version and an owner for updates.

**An explicit agreement on who updates each device**, with a name and a deadline. If the
vendor charges to install a free update, that argument needs having before the advisory,
not after.

**Monitoring of outbound traffic from edge devices.** It was the signal that worked
best: downloads initiated by the device itself, and anomalous volume heading out.

**An alert on configuration being sent outbound.** It is reconnaissance and it is
observable without knowing which vulnerability was used.

**A rehearsal of island operation.** Know in advance how long the plant can operate
without a connection, and find that number in an exercise rather than in an incident.

**Take part in whatever sector mechanism exists.** This case is the practical
demonstration that some attacks are only visible from above, and that no company can see
them alone.
