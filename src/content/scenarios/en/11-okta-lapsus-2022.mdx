## Executive summary

In January 2022, somebody obtained remote access to the machine of a person working in
customer support, at a company contracted by Okta, an identity provider thousands of
organisations rely on to authenticate their people.

**On 20 January the Okta security team received an alert**: a new factor had been added
to that support person's account, and that factor was a password. The detection worked,
and it worked on the fourth day of a five-day access window.

Okta's customers found out on **22 March**, when the LAPSUS$ group published
screenshots on an open channel.

That two-month gap is the case, and it is worth saying up front what it is not. **It is
not a case about a detection that failed.** The detection existed and it was early.
What took two months was everything that comes after detecting: obtaining the forensic
report from the contracted company, understanding the scope, and telling whoever
depended on the service.

For an analyst working at one of those customer organisations, the question this case
poses is one none of the previous ten raises: **what does a SOC do when the incident is
not its own, the information arrives late, is partial, and the parties do not agree with
each other?**

**Key metrics:**

- Access window to the support machine: 16 to 21 January 2022, five days
- Alert at the identity provider: 20 January 2022
- Public disclosure: 22 March 2022, from the group's own post
- Distance between the alert and notifying customers: around two months
- Initial estimate of potentially affected customers: around 2.5 per cent
- Primary techniques: T1199, T1591.002, T1621, T1598.004, plus T1451 in the Mobile matrix

<Warn />**Real case, with sources that do not agree.** The technical facts and dates
come from Okta's publications on the January 2022 compromise, the Cyber Safety Review
Board report of August 2023, and the LAPSUS$ entry in MITRE ATT&CK. On several points —
the actual scope, what was known and when — the parties gave different accounts at
different moments, and some figures were revised afterwards. Where something comes from
one party's account, the text says so.

## Context: what it means to depend on an identity provider

An organisation that uses an external identity provider delegates the most important
question in its security: **who each person coming in is.** That provider authenticates
staff against email, business applications, the cloud and everything else.

The delegation is sensible. A specialist provider does that job better than most
organisations, and concentrating authentication in one place lets policy be applied
once.

But it moves the trust boundary, and it moves it further out than the diagram usually
shows:

```
your organisation
    └── trusts → your identity provider
                     └── trusts → its support suppliers
                                      └── trusts → its staff's machines
```

Each arrow is a contractual relationship and none appears in your asset inventory. This
case is about the third arrow, which is the one almost nobody looks at.

And there is an asymmetry worth stating once: **the incident happens on a system you do
not administer, it is investigated by people you do not know, and the consequences land
in your organisation.** Your ability to act is minimal; your responsibility is not.

## 1. The alert that did exist

The detail is worth dwelling on because it teaches a concrete and good detection.

According to Okta's account, on 20 January its security team was alerted that **a new
factor had been added to the account** of a support person at the contracted company,
and that **the factor was a password**.

Consider why that is an alert and not noise.

Adding an authentication factor to somebody else's account is an infrequent,
high-impact administrative action: whoever can do it can come in later. On an account
with access to customer support tooling, the impact multiplies.

It is exactly the same kind of signal as case 02: there, a device enrolled for the
second factor at 09:51. **Changes to an account's authentication mechanism are among
the few actions that almost never have a spontaneous benign explanation**, which is
why they pay off so well as a detection.

The uncomfortable conclusion of the case is not that this alert was missing. It is that
**it existed, it was handled, and customers still took two months to find out.**
Detecting is one part of the work; getting the information to whoever can act is
another, and the second does not improve on its own when the first does.

## 2. What a support account could and could not do

This point is central and is usually told badly. According to what Okta published,
support personnel **could not create or delete users, or download customer databases**.
They did have access to limited data, such as tickets and lists of users, and to
assistance tooling.

It is worth taking seriously in both directions.

**Downwards:** it is not total access. The picture of "the attacker got into the
identity provider and could do anything to any customer" is false, and repeating it
prevents understanding the real risk.

**Upwards:** a support tool that can assist with access problems is, by definition, a
tool that can act on people's authentication. That is its purpose. You do not need to be
able to download a database to do damage if you can help somebody recover access to an
account.

From that comes the question a customer can ask, and that does not depend on any
statement from the provider:

> What exactly can my provider's support personnel do to my instance? And is it recorded
> in a log I can read?

Almost no organisation knows the answer before the incident. It gets learned during,
which is the worst moment.

## 3. What a customer SOC can do without waiting for the provider

This is the most useful part of the case, and the one that turns a news story into a
procedure.

When the group's post appears and the provider still has no numbers, the temptation is
to wait. **There is no need.** A customer has its own logs, and those logs can answer
the question that matters: *did they touch my instance?*

The logic is the same one case 07 puts in writing for the cloud: **look for the
contradiction with the design.** A support action by the provider on your instance is
legitimate when you asked for it. In a window where you opened no ticket, it is not.
You can make that comparison on your own, and it does not require anybody to confirm
anything.

And there is an underlying decision worth taking early: **rotating privileged
credentials and revoking sessions does not require confirmation of impact.** It is
cheap, reversible and does not depend on the provider finishing its investigation.
Waiting for confirmation to do something that could have been done on day one is the
expensive mistake in this kind of incident.

## 4. When the figures move

The initial estimate published spoke of around 2.5 per cent of customers potentially
affected. With further investigation, the figures were revised.

A number moving during an incident is not an anomaly: **it is normal**, and it is worth
understanding why before judging it.

At first you are answering "how many could have been touched?", which is an upper bound
built on what was technically possible. Later you are answering "how many were
touched?", which requires positive evidence for each case. They are two different
questions and the second always gives a smaller number.

For a SOC communicating inside its own organisation, two practical rules follow.

**Label each number with the question it answers.** "Up to X customers may have been
reached" is not the same as "X customers were reached", and the difference is lost on
the first retelling if it is not written down.

**Do not adopt the provider's figure as your own.** A customer does not need to know how
many organisations were affected in total: it needs to know whether its own was. And
that, as we saw, is answered with your own logs.

## 5. How this group gets in, and why it matters for the case

MITRE documents LAPSUS$ techniques under `G1004`, and two of them explain the structure
of the incident better than any narrative.

**`T1591.002`, Gather Victim Org Information: Business Relationships.** The group
gathers detailed knowledge of an organisation's supply chain relationships. That is not
a reconnaissance detail: **it is the design of the attack.** They look for who provides
services to whom, in order to come in where trust is already granted.

**`T1199`, Trusted Relationship.** MITRE states it plainly: the group accessed
internet-facing identity providers, including Azure Active Directory and Okta, to reach
specific organisations.

The rest of the documented repertoire shares a trait worth seeing together:

| Technique | What they do |
|---|---|
| `T1598.004` Spearphishing Voice | Call the help desk to have a privileged account's credentials reset |
| `T1684.001` Social Engineering: Impersonation | Pose as legitimate people using information gathered beforehand |
| `T1621` MFA Request Generation | Flood with second factor prompts until somebody approves |
| `T1586.002` Compromise Accounts | Pay employees, suppliers and partners for credentials |
| `T1597.002` Purchase Technical Data | Buy credentials and session cookies on forums |
| `T1451` SIM Card Swap | Take over the victim's phone number |

None of them is a software vulnerability. **They all target people and processes**: the
help desk, the person who approves a prompt, the employee who accepts an offer, the
telephone operator who transfers a line.

The Cyber Safety Review Board reached a conclusion worth quoting because it reorders
priorities: it found that **the multi-factor implementations in broad use today are not
sufficient** for most organisations, and specifically pointed to the collective failure
to account for the risks of using text messages and voice calls as a second factor.

Read alongside case 002 on this site, the message completes itself: there MFA was
bypassed with a stolen cookie; here it is bypassed by telephoning the help desk. **In
both cases the second factor was not broken: it was gone around.**

It is the second case on this site to step outside the enterprise matrix; the other is
09, with the ICS matrix. And it is not a catalogue detail: **if your second factor
arrives by text message, your security control depends on the telephone operator**,
which is not your provider, did not sign your contract and is not in your inventory.
That dependency only shows up if you look at the right matrix.

It is also worth noting where the map concentrates. Eight of the nine techniques are in
reconnaissance, resource development, initial access and credential access: **all before
getting in, or in the act of getting in.** There is no escalation, no sophisticated
lateral movement, no malware. It is the Cyber Safety Review Board's observation about
this group, and it is what makes them hard to defend against: there is nothing to patch.

## Lessons learned

**The trust boundary reaches your provider's provider.** Your organisation trusts its
identity provider, which trusts its support companies, which trust their staff's
machines. None of those relationships appears in your asset inventory, and the attacker
studies them deliberately.

**Detecting and communicating are two different capabilities.** Here the detection
worked on the fourth day of five. What took two months was obtaining a third party's
forensics, understanding the scope and giving notice. Improving detection does not
improve that.

**A customer can answer "was I touched?" with its own logs.** Support actions by the
provider on your instance in a window where you opened no ticket: you verify that
contradiction on your own, without waiting for anyone.

**Rotating credentials and revoking sessions does not require confirmation of impact.**
It is cheap, reversible and can be done on day one. Waiting for confirmation to do it is
the expensive mistake in this kind of incident.

**Figures moving is normal.** First you answer how many *could* have been reached, then
how many were. They are different questions. Every number has to carry its own, or the
distinction is lost on the first retelling.

**This group does not exploit software, it exploits processes.** Help desk, prompt
approvals, employees willing to sell access, operators who transfer a line. There is
nothing to patch, which is why the controls that work are process controls: identity
verification at the help desk, a second factor resistant to approval by habit, and less
trust in the phone number.

**If your second factor arrives by text message, it depends on your telephone
operator.** Which is not your provider, did not sign your contract and is not in your
inventory. The board that reviewed this activity concluded that broadly used
multi-factor implementations are not sufficient, and pointed specifically at text and
voice.

---

## Recommendations

- Inventory, for each critical provider, what its support personnel can do to your
  instance, and whether those actions land in a log you can read
- Your own detection of administrative actions initiated by provider support, compared
  against your own open tickets
- An alert for changes to an account's authentication mechanism: factors added, reset or
  removed
- A written response procedure for a third-party incident, with the actions that do not
  require confirmation of impact identified in advance
- Contractual notification clauses with a deadline, and a right to receive the forensic
  report rather than just a summary
- A second factor resistant to approval by habit and bound to the origin, instead of
  text message codes or approve-or-deny prompts
- Reinforced identity verification at the help desk for credential and second factor
  resets, using a method that does not rely on data that can be looked up
- Instance log retention sufficient to investigate a window of months backwards, not
  days
