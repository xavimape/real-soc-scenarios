## Executive summary

The nine previous cases begin in one of three ways: an alert in the queue, a tip-off
from outside, or the impact already done. This one begins in a fourth way, and it is
the only one the team controls: **somebody sat down and looked.**

A scheduled query over name resolution traffic — the kind that runs on Tuesday
mornings without expecting to find anything — returned a domain with **14,302 distinct
subdomains queried in seven days**. Second on the list had forty.

No alert had fired, and rightly so: the channel used DNS, which is permitted in every
organisation because without it nothing works. The data volume was tiny. There was no
malicious file on disk for an antivirus to recognise.

What there was, was a **shape**: queries of even length, never repeated, at intervals
of forty-something seconds, seven days running, Sunday included. A person's traffic
does not have that shape. A program's does.

The chain was cut at the seventh link, which is the last one and the only irreversible
one. **Not a single file left.**

**Key metrics:**

- Source of the detection: scheduled hunt, no prior alert
- Distinct subdomains in 7 days: 14,302, against 40 for the second domain on the list
- Interval between queries: 40 to 44 seconds, median 42
- Compromised machines: 3, all three from the same email
- Data exfiltrated: none
- Time from the finding to containment: 5 hours 10 minutes
- Primary techniques: T1566.001, T1204.002, T1071.004, T1572

<Warn />**Synthetic scenario.** The organisation, the machines and the logs were built
for the exercise. IP addresses come from the ranges RFC 5737 reserves for
documentation and the domains use the `.example` TLD from RFC 2606. The techniques and
the detection logic are documented by MITRE.

## Context: why DNS

A covert channel needs a protocol that always gets out. The candidate list is short,
and DNS is first for a structural reason: **if DNS does not get out, nothing works**, so
no organisation blocks it.

The mechanism is simple to understand. The attacker controls a domain and its
authoritative name server. The implant on the victim machine encodes what it wants to
say inside the name it queries:

```
[encoded data].tunnel.malicious-example.example
```

That query does not go straight to the attacker: it is resolved by the organisation's
internal resolver, which in turn asks upstream until it reaches the domain's
authoritative server, which belongs to the attacker. That is where the message
arrives. The answer comes back the same way, inside a TXT record or a made-up address.

Three properties define the case, and they follow from that.

**It is slow and loud in count.** Each query carries a few dozen bytes. Getting a
document out over this channel takes thousands of queries, and that leaves an enormous
footprint in count even while being tiny in volume.

**It crosses almost any control.** There is no outbound connection from the machine to
the attacker's server: there is a name query, which is exactly what is expected of any
machine.

**Content is no use for detecting it.** It is encoded and often encrypted. What works
is the shape: how many distinct names, how long, with what regularity.

## 1. The query that found the case

There was no alert. There was a scheduled query that runs every Tuesday and almost
always returns nothing interesting.

```sql
-- Parent domains ordered by number of distinct subdomains, 7 days
SELECT
    parent_domain,
    COUNT(DISTINCT queried_name) AS unique_subdomains,
    COUNT(*)                     AS total_queries,
    COUNT(DISTINCT client)       AS distinct_clients
FROM dns_queries
WHERE date >= CURRENT_DATE - 7
GROUP BY parent_domain
ORDER BY unique_subdomains DESC
LIMIT 20;
```

The result:

```
parent_domain                     unique_subdomains  queries  clients
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
tunnel.malicious-example.example         14,302      14,302        1
cdn-images.supplier.example                  40       8,911      612
telemetry.antivirus.example                  31      21,004      874
...
```

The first three rows say everything needed, and they are worth reading together.

**The first column separates row one from the rest by a factor of three hundred.** That
is not a difference of degree, it is a difference of kind.

**The ratio between subdomains and queries is exactly 1 to 1.** Every name was queried
once. That does not happen to any legitimate service: a content delivery network has
few names queried many times, because the purpose of a name is for people to come back
to it. A name used once and never again is not a name, it is a message.

**The last column confirms it.** The legitimate domains on the list are queried by
hundreds of clients. This one, by a single client.

One thing about the method is worth pausing on: **the query is not looking for anything
bad.** It has no list of suspicious domains and no malware family signature. It orders
by a structural property and looks at the extreme. That is the difference between
looking for the known and looking for the odd, and only one of the two finds something
nobody has seen before.

Each block in that table contributes something different, and it is worth knowing which
one carries weight.

**The rhythm is the strongest signal and the hardest to fake.** Forty-two seconds
median, sustained for seven days, Saturday and Sunday included. No human activity has
that regularity, and no machine switched off at night produces it. The arithmetic
closes on its own: seven days are 604,800 seconds, and at forty-two per query that
gives about 14,400. 14,302 were observed.

**The low proportion of NXDOMAIN says there is somebody on the other side.** A domain
generated at random by an algorithm produces a great many "does not exist" responses,
because most of the names it tries are not registered. Here almost everything resolves:
the authoritative server answers what it is asked, which is exactly what a command and
control server does.

**The domain's reputation contributed nothing, and this is the point most worth
underlining.** Registered three weeks earlier, unclassified in every public source, on
no block list. A threat intelligence feed would not have said a word about this domain.
**What gave it away was its behaviour, not its identity.**

## 3. The resolver hides the origin

Here is the practical obstacle that trips up a lot of DNS investigation, and that is
worth knowing before meeting it.

In the perimeter logs, all 14,302 queries come from `192.0.2[.]10`. That address does
not belong to the compromised machine: **it is the organisation's internal resolver.**

It is the system's normal behaviour. Machines do not query the internet directly: they
ask the resolver, and the resolver asks upstream. From outside, the whole organisation
has a single face.

The consequence is uncomfortable: **with perimeter telemetry you can prove there is a
tunnel and you cannot know which machine it comes from.** And without the machine,
there is nothing to contain.

What solves the problem is the resolver's own query log, which does hold the address of
the client that asked. In this case it existed. When it does not, the investigation
stalls at the worst possible moment, and the fix is not a new tool but a logging
decision that had to have been made earlier.

From that comes a rule that applies well beyond DNS:

> **Every aggregation point erases the origin.** The name resolver, the web proxy, the
> outbound mail server, address translation. At each of them, the log that matters is
> the one from the aggregation point itself, not the one from what leaves afterwards.

The resolver log also produced the fact that changed the scope: **it was not one
machine, it was three.**

## 4. Deciding when to cut

With the tunnel confirmed and three machines identified, at 11:25, the impulse is to
block the domain immediately. The team did not do so until 14:30, and that interval
deserves an explanation because it is where the case is played out.

Blocking the domain first has three problems.

**It does not remove the attacker.** The implant is still on the three machines. It
loses the channel, not the presence. Many implants have a second channel planned for
exactly this.

**It warns.** A channel cut abruptly is an unmistakable signal to whoever is on the
other side. If there is still something the operator wanted to do, they will try it now.

**It closes the window of knowledge.** While the channel is alive you can see what it
queries, how often, and whether new machines appear. Switched off, that information
disappears.

And yet waiting also has a cost, and it is not theoretical: every minute the channel
stays open is a minute in which the operator can start taking data out.

**The criterion that resolves the tension is not improvised. It is this:**

> If there is bulk transfer under way, you cut now and investigate afterwards. If it is
> a command channel with no bulk transfer, there is a bounded window to close the scope,
> and that window is defined in advance and against a clock.

In this case the numbers said command channel: 14,302 queries of a few dozen bytes over
seven days are a few hundred kilobytes in total, almost all outbound-direction traffic.
No document is leaving through that. With that fact, a three-hour window to close the
scope was a defensible decision and not a delay.

And when it was cut, it was cut **all at once**: the three machines isolated, the domain
blocked at the resolver and the credentials reset, in the same manoeuvre. It is the same
logic case 09 puts in writing: **with people on the other side, the partial action warns
without taking away capability.**

---

## 5. What was done, in order

1. **Isolation of the three machines** simultaneously, at the network level, keeping
   them powered on for memory evidence.
2. **Blocking the domain at the resolver**, not only at the perimeter, so a forgotten
   machine cannot resolve it either.
3. **Resetting the credentials** used on those machines and revoking their sessions.
4. **Hunting for the same persistence** across the rest of the estate, by the task that
   starts with the session rather than by the file hash.
5. **Retroactive hunting for the same pattern** across ninety days of telemetry, to rule
   out that the channel existed earlier under a different domain.
6. **Reviewing which file shares** were accessible from those three machines, to know
   what was within reach even if nothing was taken.

Step five is the one most often missing. Finding a domain does not close the question:
the question is since when, and only looking backwards with the pattern answers that —
not with the indicator.

And step six marks the difference between "they took nothing" and "we did not see them
take anything". Here the scope of what was accessible was documented, even with no
evidence of transfer.

## 6. The detection that remains

The hunt becomes two rules, and the second is the one that counts.

```sql
-- Rule 1: subdomain cardinality per parent domain, daily
SELECT parent_domain, COUNT(DISTINCT queried_name) AS unique_names
FROM dns_queries
WHERE date = CURRENT_DATE
GROUP BY parent_domain
HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT queried_name) > 500;

-- Rule 2: interval regularity per client-domain pair
-- A very low standard deviation of the interval between queries
-- describes automation, not human behaviour.
```

Three warnings about those rules, so they do not read as a closed solution.

**The first rule has legitimate and frequent false positives.** Content delivery
networks, antivirus reputation services and cloud product telemetry use high-cardinality
subdomains by design. The rule needs an exception list, **and that list needs periodic
review**: every entry is a place where the detection stops looking.

**The second rule is the one that ages well.** Cardinality is evaded by spreading the
traffic across several domains. Interval regularity is more expensive to avoid, because
the attacker has to introduce real variation, and variation costs them predictability.

**A fixed threshold is calibrated against the environment itself.** Five hundred
subdomains a day may be a lot or a little depending on the size of the organisation, and
the right number comes from looking at the real distribution for a few weeks, not from
copying it out of an article.

## Lessons learned

**A fourth way of discovering an incident: looking for it.** Alert, external tip-off,
completed impact, and hunt. It is the only one of the four that depends on the team, and
the only one that can find something nobody wrote a signature for.

**The query that found the case was not looking for anything bad.** It ordered by a
structural property and looked at the extreme. Looking for the known finds the known;
looking for the odd finds what nobody has seen.

**The domain's reputation contributed nothing.** Registered three weeks earlier,
unclassified, on no lists. Behaviour gave it away, not identity.

**Every aggregation point erases the origin.** The resolver showed a single address for
the whole organisation. Without the resolver's own log there is a confirmed tunnel and
no machine to contain.

**When to cut is a decision with a prior criterion, not a hunch.** Transfer under way,
cut now. Command channel with no volume, there is a bounded window to close the scope,
defined in advance and against a clock.

**You cut all at once.** Three machines, domain and credentials in the same manoeuvre.
The partial action warns without taking away capability.

**The ATT&CK map of a contained incident ends earlier, and that is where the measure
is.** No exfiltration column. How far across the columns the other side got says more
than any mean time to respond.

**Cutting the last link was enough, and that does not contradict cutting early.** Six
phases reached is a lot, and all of them were repairable. The seventh is not. Cutting
early is better; cutting the last one is what decides the severity.

---

## Recommendations

- Query logging at the internal resolver itself, with the client address, retained long
  enough for a ninety-day retroactive search
- Scheduled hunting by subdomain cardinality and by interval regularity, with analyst
  time allocated in advance
- An exception list for the cardinality rule, with periodic review and an owner: every
  entry is a place where the detection stops looking
- Thresholds calibrated against the environment's own real distribution, measured over
  weeks
- A written criterion for when to cut a command channel, with the volume condition
  explicit and a maximum window
- Retroactive hunting for the pattern, not the indicator, on any covert channel finding
- Document the scope of what was accessible from the compromised machines, even with no
  evidence of transfer
- Domain blocking at the resolver as well as the perimeter
