## Executive summary

Between March and June 2020, anyone who updated their network monitoring platform
installed, along with the update, an implant that handed access to a foreign
intelligence service.

The update was not tampered with after leaving the factory. **The malicious code was
added inside the factory, during compilation, and came out signed with the vendor's
legitimate certificate.** To every control on the customer's side — digital signature,
vendor reputation, hash against the one the vendor published — the file was exactly
what it claimed to be, because it was.

The previous fifteen cases start from something an analyst can look at: an alert, a
log, an anomaly. This one starts from the opposite. **The question here is what a SOC
does when the attacker came in through the one path that was authorised not to raise
suspicion.**

And the incident itself answered it, in the least dramatic way possible. Nobody
detected the implant for what it was. Someone looked at a severity-zero alert — a
second device registered against an employee's second factor — picked up the phone and
asked. The person said it had not been them.

**Key metrics:**

- First access to the vendor's network: September 2019
- Malicious code inside the product: 26 March 2020
- Removed by the attackers themselves: June 2020
- Public disclosure: 13 December 2020
- Time between insertion and disclosure: about nine months
- Implant dwell before its first communication: 12 to 14 days
- Main techniques: T1195.002, T1554, T1553.002, T1071.004, T1606.002

<Warn />**Real case, with figures that do not match across sources.** The technical
facts come from CISA advisory AA20-352A, from SolarWinds' publications on its own
investigation, from Mandiant's analysis of SUNBURST and from CrowdStrike's analysis of
SUNSPOT. On how many organisations received the compromised version and how many moved
to the second stage, the sources give different numbers; the disagreement is recorded
below, without picking one.

## Context: why this piece and not another

The compromised product was a network monitoring platform. It is worth pausing on what
that means before looking at the implant, because it explains why the attacker chose
this door and not another.

A tool that monitors infrastructure needs, in order to do its job:

```
privileged credentials       to query network devices, servers and services
unrestricted network reach   to get to everything it has to watch
antivirus exclusions         because its behaviour looks like an attacker's
outbound internet access     to update itself and to report
```

Each of those four things is correctly granted. **Together they describe the most
appealing asset on the network**, and they describe something less comfortable: a
console from which, if someone controls it, almost no movement looks odd.

The attacker did not need to escalate privileges. They were granted at installation.

## 1. Where the compromise happened

The distinction that organises the whole case is **where** the code was added.

A product is compiled from source code. The result of that compilation is signed and
published. If someone alters the file after signing, the signature stops validating and
any control catches it.

Here the implant was installed on the system that compiles. It watched the build
process, waited for the component it cared about to be compiled and swapped the source
for its own version, only for that instant. The developer who triggered the build saw
nothing different. **The source code in the repository stayed clean, and the binary
came out with the implant inside.**

Then the chain went on as normal: the binary was signed with the legitimate
certificate, published on the vendor's site and distributed as an update.

<Fail />The digital signature validated: the vendor placed it over a file that was
already compromised.

<Fail />The hash matched the published one: it was the file the vendor really compiled
and published.

<Pass />The only thing left odd was the later behaviour, and the implant was designed
so that it would not look odd either.

That is the point worth taking away: **a digital signature certifies origin, not
intent.** It says who compiled and published that file, not whether what is inside is
what its author believes it is.

---

## 2. How it avoided looking like what it was

The implant did nothing for the first twelve to fourteen days, and it picked the exact
threshold at random within that range.

It is worth thinking about what that detail is designed against:

- **Against automated analysis.** A file detonated in a sandbox is watched for a few
  minutes. Twelve days later nobody is looking.
- **Against temporal correlation.** By the time the implant wakes up, the update
  install is two weeks back and outside the window an analyst reviews when looking for
  the cause of something.
- **Against the pattern, the random threshold.** If they all woke on the same day, the
  spike would be visible. Spread across a two-day window, there is no spike.

The first communication went to a subdomain of a single domain, over DNS, in a format
that mimicked the product's own protocol. A monitoring product querying odd names and
talking to its vendor is exactly what one expects from a monitoring product.

And the answer to that query decided everything: most installations received nothing
further. **The attacker used the first stage as a census, not as access.**

The third block carries the most weight and gets looked at the least. CISA documented
that this adversary, once inside, stole the material an organisation uses to sign
authentication assertions and **minted valid credentials for any user and any
service.** With that it no longer needs the implant: it walks through the front door,
carrying authentic documents issued by the organisation itself.

Access like that does not generate a suspicious sign-in. It generates a session that
simply exists. The only way to see it is to ask about the absence: **there is
authenticated activity from this account — where is the sign-in that produced it?**

That question — looking for the event that is missing rather than the one that is extra
— is the same one case 010 uses for the DNS tunnel, applied to identity.

## 4. The figures that do not match

The number of affected organisations gets repeated a lot and holds up poorly.

| Source | What it says |
| --- | --- |
| Vendor communications | Around 18,000 customers downloaded a version carrying the implant |
| Response analysis | Of that total, a much smaller subset received the second stage |
| Figures in circulation | That subset was described as "fewer than a hundred" and also as "a few hundred" |

**I am not picking one.** The difference between "fewer than a hundred" and "a few
hundred" is not cosmetic: it changes by a factor of three or more the estimate of how
many organisations had an intruder inside, and no public source I have seen settles it
with evidence.

What does hold up, and is what matters to an analyst, is the shape: **a very large
majority received the implant and were never targeted; a small minority were the real
target.** Having installed the compromised version and having been attacked are two
distinct facts, and conflating them leads to two expensive mistakes. One is treating
the whole organisation as compromised and paralysing it. The other is concluding
nothing happened because the implant never advanced, without having verified it.

The verification is the DNS query from the block above. It is cheap and it answers
exactly that question.

---

## 5. How it was found, which is the part that gets forgotten

None of the capabilities we usually buy for this found it. Not the antivirus, not
endpoint detection, not threat intelligence, not the vendor review.

It was found by **an alert most SOCs classify as noise**: a second device was
registered for an employee's second authentication factor.

It is an alert that fires every day for legitimate reasons. Someone changes phone,
someone adds a tablet, someone reinstalls the app. In almost every case, closing it as
benign is correct.

What happened that time is that someone did the one-minute verification the situation
calls for: **they asked the person.** The person said they had registered no device.
From there, the question became how anyone had obtained those credentials, and that
question ended at the monitoring server.

It is worth saying plainly, because it is the most usable lesson in the case: the
largest known espionage campaign of the decade opened up because a routine ticket was
not closed from memory. Case 014 on this site is about how a false positive gets closed
well; this one is its exact reverse, and both say the same thing. **The value is not in
the alert, it is in the verification.**

## Analysis frameworks

**I am not including the kill chain.** All seven links were completed, and a chain with
seven rows in red does not teach where to cut, which is what the framework is for. Case
004 already shows a complete chain, and there the point is that controls existed and
failed one by one.

Here the reason is different and worth stating in prose: **the controls worked as
designed, and the design did not account for this path.** A control that validates
signatures did its job correctly by accepting a valid signature. There is no link to
point at as the failure, and that absence is precisely the case's finding.

The chain was cut outside the chain: nine months later, at another organisation,
through an identity anomaly.

## Lessons learned

**A digital signature answers a smaller question than it appears to.** It certifies
that a file left a given vendor unaltered afterwards. It says nothing about whether the
vendor was compromised before signing. It is a valid control and worth having; what is
not worth doing is asking it for a guarantee it does not give.

**Administration software is critical network infrastructure, and is rarely treated as
such.** Tools that monitor, back up, distribute patches or manage endpoints hold broad
credentials, total reach and detection exclusions. They deserve the same scrutiny as a
domain controller.

**Installing the compromised version and having been attacked are two distinct facts.**
Responding to a notice like this starts by determining which of the two applies, and
that determination is cheap.

**The detection that worked was identity, not threat detection.** When the attacker
comes in through a trusted channel, what is left to see them is their behaviour as a
user: a device that appears, a session with no origin, a permission nobody asked for.

**A routine alert is worth whatever its verification is worth.** Without the phone
call, that notice would have been closed as benign, and closing it that way was
statistically reasonable.

---

## Recommendations

**An inventory of privileged software.** Which administration tools run on the network,
under which accounts, with what reach and with which detection exclusions. Without that
list, a vendor compromise notice cannot be answered.

**Segment those tools' outbound access.** A monitoring server needs to talk to the
internal network and to its vendor's update site. It does not need to talk to the
internet at large, and that restriction does not break the product.

**Log and review DNS from servers, not just from workstations.** The first stage was a
name query from a server. It is the only artefact that was available across those nine
months.

**Watch the material that signs authentication assertions** and the sessions that have
no sign-in to explain them.

**Treat second-factor changes as verifiable, not informational.** A new device is
confirmed with the person over a different channel. It is the cheapest verification in
the catalogue and in this case it was the only one that worked.
