## Executive summary

In 2019, someone obtained the credit card applications Capital One held in the cloud.
The Department of Justice press release describes the route in a single sentence:
**"The intrusion occurred through a misconfigured web application firewall that
enabled access to the data."**

That sentence hides the step that makes the case interesting. The firewall did not
hand over the data: it handed over **credentials**. A manipulated web request made it
query the metadata service of the instance it was running on, and that service
returned the temporary credentials of the role attached to the machine. With that
borrowed identity, the storage was listed and copied.

And there is the question that organises the whole case: **why did a firewall, whose
job is to inspect HTTP traffic, have permission to read the credit applications of a
hundred million people?**

The intrusion was not detected by Capital One. It was detected by a stranger who saw
a post on GitHub and sent an email, on 17 July 2019.

**Key metrics:**

- Discovery: by external tip-off, on 17 July 2019
- Bank's confirmation of the intrusion: 19 July 2019
- OCC civil money penalty: 80 million dollars, 6 August 2020
- Failures the regulator put in writing: network controls, data loss prevention and
  **effective dispositioning of alerts**
- Malware involved: none
- Primary techniques: T1190, T1552.005, T1078.004, T1530

<Warn />**Real case.** Everything technical and chronological here comes from the
sources cited at the end: the Department of Justice criminal complaint and press
release, and the OCC orders of 6 August 2020. Where a statement comes from later

<Warn />**On the figures.** The numbers that circulated at the time speak of around a
hundred million people in the United States and some six million in Canada. The
Department of Justice press release I cite does not state them, and neither do the OCC
orders. I mention them because they are public, not because I verified them against an
official source.

## Context: what the instance metadata service is

This is the piece to understand before anything else, because the rest of the case
follows from it.

Every virtual machine in a public cloud can query a local service that tells it about
itself: its identifier, its region, its network configuration. In AWS that service
lives at a link-local address, `169.254.169.254`, and only answers from inside the
instance.

Among the things it returns there is something far more valuable than the region: **if
the machine has an identity role attached, the metadata service hands over temporary
credentials for that role.**

That is a good idea, and it is worth saying why before criticising it. Without that
mechanism, an application that needs to talk to storage would have to carry a key
written into a config file, which somebody copies, which ends up in a repository,
which never rotates. The metadata service replaces that permanent key with credentials
that expire on their own and that nobody writes down anywhere.

The price is this consequence:

**Any component that can be induced to issue an HTTP request on the machine's behalf
can ask for those credentials.**

And that is exactly what an SSRF — server-side request forgery — does: convince an
application to issue a request wherever the attacker wants. On a physical machine an
SSRF is usually a nuisance of limited reach. On a machine with a role attached, **an
SSRF is identity theft.**

The distinction worth taking away: the attacker did not take data using a flaw in the
storage. They took data using a legitimate identity that the storage had every right
to serve.

from step two onwards, takes place inside what the system considers authorised
behaviour.

## 2. The question that defines the case

The first three steps are a technical flaw and are fixable with a patch or a
configuration. The third is different: it is a design decision, and it is the only one
that explains the magnitude.

**A web application firewall inspects HTTP traffic.** It looks at headers, bodies,
parameters; it decides whether a request passes or is blocked. To do that job it does
not need to read a single customer record.

If that instance's role had held only what the firewall needs, the SSRF would have
handed over credentials for something useless. The incident would exist all the same —
the SSRF still has to be fixed — but it would be an audit finding, not a breach
affecting a hundred million people.

Hence the formulation worth taking away: **least privilege does not reduce the
probability of an incident, it reduces its radius.** It does not stop them getting in;
it decides how far they reach once inside. And that is why it is the control that pays
most in the cloud, where nearly everything an attacker does after the first step is
authenticated API calls.

Put the other way round, which is how it looks in daily work: **every extra permission
on a role is a decision about how big the next incident will be.**

## 3. How it was detected

Here is the uncomfortable fact, and it is in the official release without decoration:
on 17 July 2019, **a GitHub user who saw the post alerted Capital One** to the
possibility of a data theft. On 19 July the bank determined there had been an
intrusion, and contacted the FBI.

Four months of access, and the channel that worked was an email inbox.

Two things follow for a SOC, and neither of them is "you need luck".

**The security contact inbox is a detection channel, not a formality.** If it exists,
it needs an owner, a committed response time and a direct route to somebody who can
open an incident. An organisation without that inbox, or with one nobody reads, loses
the tip-off that in this case was the only one that arrived.

**An external detection is always late.** It depends on the attacker talking, on
somebody reading it and on that somebody bothering to get in touch. It is not a
control: it is a stroke of luck with goodwill attached.

## 4. What the regulator wrote

This is the part of the case most useful to an analyst, and it is uncommon: there is
an official document that states, in banking supervision language, what the SOC was
missing.

The OCC cease and desist order, dated 6 August 2020, records in its findings:

> In or around 2015, the Bank failed to establish effective risk assessment processes
> prior to migrating its information technology operations to the cloud operating
> environment. The Bank also failed to establish appropriate risk management for the
> cloud operating environment, including appropriate design and implementation of
> certain network security controls, adequate data loss prevention controls, and
> **effective dispositioning of alerts**.

And it adds two more findings:

> The Bank's internal audit failed to identify numerous control weaknesses and gaps in
> the cloud operating environment. Internal audit also did not effectively report on
> and highlight identified weaknesses and gaps to the Audit Committee.

> For certain concerns raised by internal audit, the Board failed to take effective
> actions to hold management accountable.

Three sentences worth reading slowly.

**"Effective dispositioning of alerts."** It is the only one of the three technical
failures that describes an analyst's daily work. And the verb matters: it does not say
alerts were missing, it says they were not effectively dispositioned. In this site's
vocabulary it is the same failure cases 004 and 006 show from the inside, written here
from the outside by a regulator.

**Internal audit found things and they did not make it upstairs.** The problem was not
a lack of findings but their transport. A finding not communicated in a way that
conveys its materiality is, for practical purposes, a finding that does not exist.

**The Board did not hold management accountable.** That is to say: some of the material
did make it up, and nothing happened. That is the most expensive of the three failures
and the one no technical control repairs.

The order goes on to require, among the corrective actions, developing "comprehensive
processes to prevent and detect unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information sent
outside the Bank's technology environment". Translated: **there was no egress
detection.** Step four of the chain could copy everything with nothing watching the
volume going out.

## 5. What changed afterwards

In November 2019 AWS released a second version of the metadata service, IMDSv2, aimed
precisely at this form of indirect access. Two changes matter here:

**Session with a token.** A `GET` to the service address is no longer enough: you first
have to request a token with a `PUT` and then present it in a header. Most SSRFs only
manage to make the application issue a simple `GET`, so the bar goes up considerably.

**Hop limit.** The service's response goes out with an IP-level hop limit that defaults
to 1. The AWS documentation describes it precisely by its consequences in containers:
with the limit at 1, a request coming from a container gets no response, because
reaching the container counts as an additional hop. That side effect is the defence:
**it cuts out intermediaries**, which is the position from which a proxy or a firewall
would make the request.

The practical caveat is worth stating, because it is where this fails in real life:
IMDSv2 only protects if it is **required**. An instance configured in optional mode
accepts both versions, and an attacker who can choose will use the first. The check is
not "do we have IMDSv2 available?" but "how many instances have it in required mode?".

And it is worth keeping in view what IMDSv2 does **not** fix: if the role still holds
excessive permissions, any other route to those credentials produces the same result
again. The metadata service was the path; the permission was the problem.

## 6. What detection this calls for

Three concrete detections come out of the case, and all three live in the cloud audit
log, not in an antivirus.

**Instance role credentials used from outside the cloud.** This is the strongest of the
three and deserves an explanation. The credentials the metadata service hands over are
meant to be used by the instance. If calls signed with those credentials show up from
an address that is not the infrastructure's, something took them out of there. It is
not an indicator: it is a contradiction with the design, and that is why it does not
age.

**Access to the metadata service from a component that should not need it.** A firewall
or a proxy asking for role credentials is anomalous by the definition of its function.

**Bulk listing and reading of object storage by a role that never does it.** This
requires a per-role baseline, just as case 006 required one per server. Without a
baseline, a full listing of repositories is one more valid call.

Two observations about that table.

The first: **T1078.004 appears under privilege escalation, not initial access.** It is
the same identifier as in cases 02 and 05, used with a different tactic. An ATT&CK
technique does not belong to a single tactic: it describes an action, and the tactic
says what that action was for at that moment. Here the borrowed role grants more
permission than whoever arrived over the web had, so the correct reading is escalation.

The second: **none of the four techniques involves malicious software.** No implant, no
command and control channel, no hash to look for. It is the same trait as case 006 from
another angle, and it explains why the defence that applies here is about identity and
permissions, not about endpoints.

## Lessons learned

**An SSRF in the cloud is identity theft.** The request does not take data: it takes
the credentials of the machine's role. That changes entirely how the severity of that
class of finding should be assessed in an application review.

**Least privilege does not prevent the incident, it decides its size.** The SSRF existed
regardless. What turned a web flaw into a mass breach was that the firewall's role could
read the storage. Every extra permission is a decision made in advance about the radius
of the next incident.

**After the first step, the whole attack is valid calls.** Valid credentials, existing
role, granted permissions. There is no operation to reject, so detection has to lean on
design contradictions and on baselines, not on errors.

**The security inbox is a detection channel.** Here it was the only one that worked. It
needs an owner, a response time and a direct route to the SOC; without that, the tip-off
arrives and gets lost.

**An optional control is not a control.** IMDSv2 protects when it is in required mode.
The audit question is not whether it is available, but how many instances enforce it.

**The regulator put in writing what the SOC was missing.** Network controls, data loss
prevention and effective dispositioning of alerts. That list is worth reading for what
it is: an external description of failures that from the inside look like closed tickets
and rules nobody wrote.

**The findings existed and did not travel.** Internal audit found weaknesses and did not
manage to communicate them so that their materiality landed, and on what did make it up,
nothing followed. Detecting and failing to make it matter is one of the expensive
failures.

---

## Recommendations

- Require IMDSv2 on every instance, and audit required mode rather than availability
- Review the roles attached to network and security components: a firewall, a proxy or a
  load balancer do not need to read data storage
- Alert on instance role credentials used from addresses that are not the
  infrastructure's own
- Baseline operations per role, and alert on deviation: full listings and bulk reads
  that role never performs
- Egress detection by volume, which is the failure the regulator explicitly required
  fixing
- A security contact inbox with an owner, a committed response time and a direct route
  to somebody who can open an incident
- Risk assessment before moving operations to the cloud, with an inventory of the
  controls that are lost and the ones that have to be rebuilt
- Treat internal audit findings about the cloud as tracked items with due dates, not as
  observations
